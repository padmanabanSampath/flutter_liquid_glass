# Borrow the cube's heuristics, reject its dynamics

**Cube-solving theory improves a looped transformer in four places and misleads in a fifth. Borrow its distance-to-goal heuristics, its symmetry quotienting, its phase decomposition (loosely) and its search (offline only); invert its dynamics.** Every strong cube solver, from Korf's pattern databases to CayleyPy's >98%-optimal neural solver, pairs a cheap estimate of distance-to-solved with a search. The looped counterpart is a stage-B "remaining passes" head. It is trained on labels from running the frozen student at every pass against the teacher, posed as a per-pass hazard as Think-at-Hard and the Depth-Adaptive Transformer do, and calibrated in the opposite direction to A*, because over-estimating remaining passes is the safe error. HRM and TRM, often cited as learned halting, use their halting heads only in training and always run 16 steps at evaluation. The cube's symmetry-reduced tables have a sharper analogue in the transformer's gauge group: head permutations, FFN permutations, per-head V/O maps and RoPE-restricted Q/K maps. Aligning teacher layers into one shared basis before averaging them into the A and B blocks costs seconds per assignment problem. Published within-model tying shows aligned beats unaligned, but nobody has measured how much distillation that saves for a looped student. Phase decomposition maps onto teacher checkpoints, unique first and last layers, and a refine phase on hard tokens; block order itself (A→B→A→B vs A→A→B→B) moves quality by at most about half a point. Per-input path search finds large oracle gains in frozen LLMs, but learned routers keep only part of them. NPUs and TensorRT accept only a small menu of precompiled programs, and for a two-block student that menu can be found by exhaustive enumeration rather than MCTS. The dynamics are where the analogy inverts. Cube moves are invertible permutations whose repetition returns to the start after 4 to 1,260 steps, whereas a looped block is useful only if it contracts toward an input-conditioned fixed point, the property that makes early exit, warm starts and extra passes on hard inputs safe. Paper sites were blocked during the research, so many figures come from search excerpts and GitHub, and several were computed or simulated in the researchers' sandbox. The first step should be a training-free audit of the current student's convergence and per-input depth, because it decides which of these levers is worth building.

## Twenty moves reach 4.3×10¹⁹ positions because solvers search small quotients

### The Cayley graph is huge, shallow and concentrated near its diameter

The cube group has **43,252,003,274,489,856,000 elements**, which factors as 2²⁷·3¹⁴·5³·7²·11 ([CTF writeup](https://ctftime.org/writeup/22581)). Schreier–Sims on the six face quarter-turns reproduces that order exactly *[computed]*. Positions are the nodes of a Cayley graph, and moves are its edges. The half-turn metric (HTM) counts any face turn as one move, giving 18 generators; the quarter-turn metric (QTM) counts only 90° turns, giving 12. **God's number is 20 in HTM**, proved in July 2010 by Rokicki, Kociemba, Davidson and Dethridge. They split the positions into 2,217,093,120 sets of 19,508,428,800 each, cut the work to 55,882,296 sets using symmetry and set cover, and found solutions of 20 moves or fewer in every set with about 35 CPU-years donated by Google ([cube20.org](https://www.cube20.org/); [Brandeis copy](https://www.cs.brandeis.edu/~storer/JimPuzzles/RUBIK/Rubik3x3x3/READING/GodsNumberIs20.pdf)). In QTM the diameter is **26**, shown in 2014 by Rokicki and Davidson with about 29 CPU-years ([cube20.org/qtm](https://cube20.org/qtm/)). Counting a half-turn as one generator rather than two therefore shortens the worst case by about 23% *[derived]*; the metric is a design choice about the generator set, not a property of the puzzle.

The graph is about as efficient as an 18-regular graph can be. The number of positions at distance 0–4 is 1, 18, 243, 3,240 and 43,239 ([OEIS A080601](https://oeis.org/A080601)). Canonical move sequences grow by about **13.35× per move** *[computed]*, matching Kociemba's "about 13.3" ([OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)), so the counting bound on the diameter is about 17.45, only 2.5 below the true 20 *[computed]*. Exponential growth puts almost all positions near the diameter. Kociemba puts the chance that a random cube needs 20 moves "in the order of 1/10¹¹" ([OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)), 102,764,490 distance-20 positions were known as of April 2024 ([cube20.org](https://cube20.org/distance20s/)), and Korf found a median optimal length of 18 for random cubes ([Korf 1997](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/1997/korf1997aaai-finding)). A typical scramble is close to the worst case, a fact that will matter when the analogy breaks.

Repeating one fixed move sequence never explores. Every element has finite order: R returns to the start after 4 repetitions, R U after **105**, R U R′ U′ after 6 *[computed]*, and no element exceeds **1,260**, reached by R U2 D′ B D′ ([IBM community post](https://community.ibm.com/community/user/blogs/moloy-de1/2021/03/10/points-to-ponder); proof by [Bawden, 1980](https://www.math.rwth-aachen.de/~Martin.Schoenert/Cube-Lovers/Alan_Bawden__1260.html)). Exploration comes from changing which generator is applied at each step. Two group operations build targeted moves out of crude ones. The commutator X Y X⁻¹ Y⁻¹ cancels everywhere except where X and Y interact, so (R U R′) D (R U′ R′) D′ moves exactly three corners, a pure 3-cycle. The conjugate Y X Y⁻¹ relocates an operation without changing its cycle type *[computed]*.

### Phase solvers descend a chain of stabilizers with ever fewer moves

Thistlethwaite's algorithm solves the cube in four phases down a nested subgroup chain, using fewer moves in each phase. The researchers recomputed the subgroup orders, which confirm Jaap Scherphuis's ordering of the indices ([Jaap's Puzzle Page](https://www.jaapsch.net/puzzles/thistle.htm); [Hoey 1992](https://www.math.rwth-aachen.de/~Martin.Schoenert/Cube-Lovers/Dan_Hoey__Re__Rubik's_Cube,_the_minimax_number_of_moves.html)):

| Subgroup | Generators | Order *[computed]* | Index solved in the phase | Phase bound: original → later exact |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| G0 | U, D, L, R, F, B | 4.33×10¹⁹ | — | — |
| G1 | L, R, F, B, U2, D2 | 2.11×10¹⁶ | 2,048 (edge orientation) | 7 → 7 |
| G2 | L, R, F2, B2, U2, D2 | 19,508,428,800 | 1,082,565 | 13 → 10 |
| G3 | L2, R2, F2, B2, U2, D2 | 663,552 | 29,400 | 15 → 13 |
| G4 | identity | 1 | 663,552 | 17 → 15 |
| Total | | | Product of indices = group order | 52 → 45 moves |

Kociemba's two-phase algorithm merges these into two phases through one subgroup, G1 = ⟨U, D, R2, L2, F2, B2⟩, whose order equals Thistlethwaite's G2 *[computed]*. Phase 1 drives three coordinates to zero: corner twist (2,187 values), edge flip (2,048) and the set of UD-slice edges (495). Their product, 2,217,093,120, is exactly the index of G1. Phase 2 then uses only the 10 moves that never leave G1 and solves the remaining 1.95×10¹⁰ states ([kociemba.org](https://kociemba.org/math/twophase.htm)). In group terms, G1 is the stabilizer of the solved phase-1 coordinates, and the God's-number proof's split of 2,217,093,120 cosets times 19,508,428,800 positions is the orbit–stabilizer theorem applied to that action *[computed: the product equals the group order]*. Phases are not optimal on their own: Thistlethwaite needs up to 45 moves where 20 suffice. Kociemba closes most of the gap by continuing to enumerate phase-1 solutions and keeping the shortest total, which makes the solver an anytime algorithm. In PyPy, average solutions shrink from **20.45 moves at 0.01 s per cube to 19.33 at 0.1 s, 18.76 at 1 s and 18.37 at 10 s** ([TwophaseSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-TwophaseSolver)), against an optimal mean of about 17.8.

### Move tables, pruning tables and IDA* trade memory for search

A move table replaces permuting 20 cubies with one lookup, `next = table[coord][move]`, on a small coordinate. A pruning table stores, for every value of a coordinate or coordinate pair, the exact number of moves to the target subgroup, built once by breadth-first search outward from the target. Because it never over-estimates, it is an admissible lower bound for iterative-deepening A* (IDA*). In Kociemba's words, if you search for 17-move solutions, have generated 10 moves and "the pruning table tells you that you need at least 8 more moves", you may stop ([OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)). Korf's 1997 optimal solver took the maximum over three pattern databases (corners, and two disjoint sets of six edges), which keeps the bound admissible; it ran IDA* for about two days per cube on 1997 hardware ([Korf 1997](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/1997/korf1997aaai-finding); [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)). Kociemba's Python optimal solver uses a 794 MB symmetry-reduced table. A full depth-17 iteration generates about 2.5×10⁸ nodes, against about 1.2×10¹¹ for Korf's ~80 MB tables, roughly **480× fewer nodes** from a better heuristic, on different hardware *[derived]* ([OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)). Symmetry is the cheapest compression in all of this: min2phase stores its twist×flip table over 324 symmetry classes in 332 KB ([cuberoot.me](https://cuberoot.me/code/algorithms/min2phase)), and the God's-number proof used symmetry, together with set cover, to shrink its work about 40-fold.

### Learned solvers replace tables with networks but keep the search

Every strong learned solver is a learned distance-to-goal (or move policy) applied once per node inside a search, never a one-shot policy:

| Solver | Learned quantity | Search | Result on DeepCubeA's 1,000 test cubes (QTM) |
|---|---|---|---|
| DeepCubeA (2019) | Cost-to-go by approximate value iteration on states reached by reverse moves from the goal | Batched weighted A* | 100% solved; 60.3% optimal; 21.50 vs 20.64 optimal mean ([paper](https://cse.sc.edu/~foresta/assets/files/SolvingTheRubiksCubeWithDeepReinforcementLearningAndSearch_Final.pdf); [Takano table](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.03157)) |
| EfficientCube (TMLR 2023) | The last scramble move | Beam search | 69.6% optimal; 21.26; about 10× less training and 2× less inference than DeepCubeA ([Takano](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.03157)) |
| CayleyPy (NeurIPS 2025) | Length of the random walk that produced the state | GPU beam search, width 2²⁰ | >98% optimal, ~25.6× faster solving and up to 8.5× less training than the best prior learned solver; a one-residual-block MLP trains in 0.8 min on one H100 ([README](https://github.com/khoruzhii/cayleypy-cube); [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/pdf/d8ed8f8817a34278fae4393e7fed6e1e25d99536.pdf)) |
| Q* search (2021–2024) | Costs of all children in one forward pass | Q* | A 157-fold larger action space costs under 4× more time; up to 129× faster and 1,288× fewer nodes than A* ([arXiv 2102.04518](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.04518v2)) |
| Reference optimal solver | Pattern databases | IDA* | 20.64 average at 182 GB of memory ([Takano](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.03157)) |

Two regularities stand out. Search width buys most of the quality: in one AlphaCube example, widening the beam from 1,024 to 65,536 shortened a solution from 21 to 19 moves and a larger model reached 17 ([AlphaCube](https://github.com/kyo-takano/alphacube)). And the supervision became cheaper with each generation, from Bellman targets to the last move to raw walk length, each more compute-efficient than the last. Walk length over-estimates true distance and still works best, which suggests that consistent ranking matters more than calibrated distance *[inference]*. The researchers found no looped or weight-tied cube solver; the iteration always lives in the search loop, not the network.

The firmest theoretical bridge to looped models concerns tracking a cube, not solving one. Transformers can simulate any finite automaton with depth O(log T), and with constant depth only when its groups are solvable; non-solvable groups such as S₅ are the obstruction ([Liu et al., ICLR 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.10749)). The cube group is non-solvable *[computed]*, so following a cube through T moves needs depth that grows with T, which is exactly what looping supplies without new parameters.

The rows sort into four transfers and one inversion. Distance-to-goal transfers almost intact, with its safety direction flipped. Symmetry transfers by moving from symmetries of states to symmetries of parameters. Phase decomposition transfers loosely, as soft checkpoints and a refine phase rather than exact invariants. Search transfers offline only, because deployed graphs must be static. The dynamics invert: the cube's engine is invertibility, and a looped block needs the opposite.

## Distance-to-go makes a good exit head if it errs toward running longer

### HRM and TRM halt only in training; Think-at-Hard and the Depth-Adaptive Transformer exit at inference

The models most often cited for learned halting do not use it at inference. HRM's head is a two-logit linear layer on the first position, initialized with bias −5 so the model starts out never halting. Its "halt" target is whether the whole answer is exactly correct, and its "continue" target is a one-step bootstrap from an extra forward pass, computed with no replay buffer or target network. During evaluation the code "always use[s] max steps … to guarantee the same halting steps inside a batch" ([HRM code](https://github.com/sapientinc/HRM/blob/main/models/hrm/hrm_act_v1.py); [losses](https://github.com/sapientinc/HRM/blob/main/models/losses.py)). TRM drops the continue loss, which its author calls "totally unecessary", halts when P(correct) > 0.5, and also runs the full 16 supervision steps at test time ([TRM config](https://github.com/SamsungSAILMontreal/TinyRecursiveModels/blob/main/config/arch/trm.yaml); [trm.py](https://github.com/SamsungSAILMontreal/TinyRecursiveModels/blob/main/models/recursive_reasoning/trm.py)). In both, halting saves training compute by recycling batch slots, and a 2026 study notes that TRM's ACT "either halts too early … or too late" ([FPRM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18206)).

| Model | Label or target | Head | Used at inference | Reported effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HRM (2025) | Halt: whole answer exactly correct; continue: bootstrapped one-step value | Linear, 2 logits, position 0 | No; always 16 steps | Training-batch recycling |
| TRM (2025) | Current answer correct | Linear, halt if logit > 0 | No; always 16 steps | Same; 87.4% Sudoku-Extreme (MLP variant) |
| Depth-Adaptive Transformer (ICLR 2020) | Oracle exit from correctness (C) or likelihood (LL) | Geometric-like (continue or exit after each block) vs multinomial (pick a depth) | Yes | 34.73 BLEU at 1.42 average blocks; matches a 6-block model with under a quarter of the decoder layers ([arXiv 1910.10073](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10073)) |
| Think-at-Hard (2025; ICML 2026) | Reference model's greedy mismatch on the gold next token; BCE on `label > d` | Residual MLP over hidden states from 4 layers plus top-k logits; threshold 0.5 | Yes | 94% of tokens skip the second iteration while beating always-iterate baselines by 8–11% ([TaH](https://github.com/thu-nics/TaH)); ICML version: 3.8–4.4% at 93% skipped ([ICML](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/poster/62678)) |
| TaH2 (2026) | Online label: does another iteration lower next-token CE? | Cost-sensitive BCE | Yes | 53% better accuracy-compute slope; about +3.4 points on AIME (repo claim) ([TaH](https://github.com/thu-nics/TaH)) |

Three lessons follow. First, HRM's and TRM's heads are one-bit "solved now" classifiers, not remaining-passes predictors; any claim that they save inference compute is not supported by their code. Second, heads that re-decide after every block (geometric or hazard form) beat heads that pick a depth up front, at token level in the Depth-Adaptive Transformer. Third, the per-input distribution of needed depth is heavy-tailed and task-dependent. Running TRM to 512 steps instead of 16 raises cumulative exact solves on 1,000 hard Sudokus from **59.2% to 87.5%** (attention) and from 74.4% to 91.9% (MLP) ([arXiv 2609.26487](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26487)). On ARC-AGI-1, however, step 1 already delivers about 94% of final accuracy ([arXiv 2512.11847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.11847)), and Think-at-Hard's decider exempts 93–94% of tokens from a second pass.

### Label by running every pass, train a hazard, and calibrate toward running longer

DeepCubeA generated free, exact labels by walking backward from the goal. The student pipeline has a cheaper equivalent: with only three A→B cycles, Monte-Carlo labels from running the frozen student at every pass count are exact, so the bootstrapped targets of value iteration, and their instability (HRM uses no target network), are unnecessary *[inference]*. For each calibration input, run 1 to P+1 cycles, one beyond the deployed depth, in the spirit of CayleyPy training walk lengths up to the diameter ([CayleyPy](https://github.com/khoruzhii/cayleypy-cube)). Score each cycle against the teacher: for tokens, argmax agreement with the teacher plus KL(teacher‖student) below a tolerance; for frames, feature cosine or the task metric. Define d* as the smallest cycle after which every later cycle stays good. This "stays solved" definition absorbs flips where an extra cycle breaks a correct output. Inputs never solved by P+1 get d* equal to the cycle after which their loss stops improving, as TaH2's lookahead labels do, so the head does not learn to run hopeless inputs to the maximum *[inference]*.

Stage B then trains a hazard head with the backbone frozen. After cycle t, the head predicts y_t = 1[d* > t] with binary cross-entropy, which is TaH's and the Depth-Adaptive Transformer's "continue at depth d iff label > d". Optional cumulative outputs 1[d* > t+k] give an expected remaining-pass count after cycle 1 (the sum of continue probabilities), useful for scheduling, and ordered thresholds keep those outputs monotone *[inference]*. Class imbalance needs a positive-class weight, as TaH uses, and the bias should start toward "continue", as HRM's −5 initialization does for halting, so that training does not collapse into early exit.

The safety direction is the opposite of A*. A* stays optimal only if the heuristic never over-estimates, which is a problem because plain MSE training "regularly yields overestimations". A 2026 method fixes that with an under-estimating Bellman operator, an asymmetric loss and a post-hoc calibration offset, and reports zero admissibility violations on Lights Out, the 8-puzzle and the 2×2 cube ([arXiv 2606.04860](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04860)). For an exit head the costly error is under-estimating remaining passes, which exits early and loses quality, whereas over-estimating only spends compute. The same machinery therefore transfers with the asymmetry reversed: penalize under-prediction more, then choose an offset on held-out data that holds the premature-exit rate below a target such as 1% of tokens or frames *[inference]*. Korf's rule supplies the aggregation: take the maximum, or a high quantile, of per-token or per-patch predictions when exiting a whole sequence step or frame, which stays conservative the way a max over pattern databases stays admissible *[inference]*. A pruning-table version is also possible, storing a conservative d* per cluster of pass-1 features and replacing the MLP with a nearest-centroid lookup that suits NPUs *[inference]*.

"Run longer" is safe only if extra cycles do no harm, and in looped models they sometimes do. Think-at-Hard documents "latent overthinking", where tokens correct after one pass are revised into errors, and an oracle that skips iterations on correct tokens gains up to 7.3% ([TaH v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.08577v1)). Ouro degrades when run beyond its four trained loops ([Blayney et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.11791v1)), and HRM can lock onto a false fixed point on a Sudoku with a single blank, a failure whose repair raised Sudoku-Extreme from 54.5% to 96.9% ([Ren & Liu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10679)). The head therefore needs a zero-shot guard, exiting only when the head says stop and a cheap convergence test agrees: a small cycle-to-cycle state change, or an unchanged argmax. The guard is also the baseline to beat. A training-free rule that stops each token once its output stabilizes matched depth-8 quality at **4.94 average loops (−38%)**, while a learned linear router trained for the same job saved nothing ([Logan 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14427)).

### Put the head where the KV cache and the compiler can live with it

The head should read hidden states, not logits. HRM and TRM read one reserved position; Think-at-Hard reads four layers plus top-k logits. On a small model with a large vocabulary, though, a logit-based check after each pass reads more bytes than the pass it might save, as the compute-reduction research showed, so the device version should read a reserved or pooled token plus cheap residual features such as ‖Δh‖ per cycle *[inference]*. For per-frame vision the natural input is a pooled CLS or register token, and in video the previous frame's d* is a cheap prior worth feeding in, though no source has tested it *[inference]*.

Token-level exits in autoregressive models collide with the KV cache. A token that stops after cycle 1 leaves no cycle-2 or cycle-3 keys and values for later tokens to attend to. CALM propagates the exited token's last state into the missing slots ([CALM](https://research.google/blog/accelerating-text-generation-with-confident-adaptive-language-modeling-calm/)), and EE-LLM fills the remaining layers' KV before the step ends, for up to **1.25× over vLLM** ([arXiv 2407.20272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20272)). Ouro shows the policy must be trained in: keeping only the last loop's KV during decoding holds GSM8K at 78.85 vs 78.92, while keeping only the first loop's collapses it to 18.73 ([Ouro](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25741)). With weight-shared projections, a copied state is at least processed by the same matrices that would have processed the real one *[inference]*. For phones, browsers and Jetson at batch 1, the deployable granularity is the sequence step or the frame, with the KV policy fixed in stage A's attention masks; per-token exits belong on GPU servers. The companion compute-reduction research estimated **2.1–2.4 average passes at under 1% loss, about 1.15–1.35× real**, for a three-pass student *[inference]*. The audit below measures the ceiling directly as 1 − E[d*]/P *[derived]*.

The cube also shows why this head should stay small. A better pruning table saved about 480× in nodes because search cost is exponential in depth; a loop's cost is linear in passes, so even a perfect exit head saves at most P×, and at most 3× for this student *[derived]*. The cube's lesson that heuristic quality is worth huge tables does not carry over. The parameters belong in making passes contractive, so that a cheap, conservative head is safe.

## Block order barely matters; where blocks repeat and where phases change does

### A→B→A→B and A→A→B→B differ by half a point at most

Every controlled comparison of sharing order finds differences of a few tenths of a point, with winners that change with depth and scale:

| Study | Setting | A→A→B→B (Sequence) | A→B→A→B (Cycle) | A→B→B→A (Reverse) | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Takase & Kiyono | WMT En-De BLEU, 61M params, 6 unique layers applied 12 / 18 times | 27.13 / 27.02 | 27.04 / 27.25 | 27.08 / 27.31 | Unshared 26.57; 210M unshared 26.81 ([results](https://github.com/takase/share_layer_params/blob/main/result_table.png)) |
| MobileLLM | Zero-shot average, 125M / 350M | 45.0 / 50.2 | 45.2 / 50.7 | 44.8 / 50.1 | Shipped A→A→B→B for SRAM locality ([arXiv 2402.14905](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14905v2)) |
| Mixture-of-Recursions | Validation NLL, 135M–360M | Worst in all four settings, gap widening with depth | | | Middle-Cycle best at 360M; 3.1048 vs 3.1154 for Cycle at 135M ([MoR](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10524v3)) |
| Loopie (2026) | 20B-total MoE, 2 loops | Chosen ("layer-loop") | | | Beats compute-matched vanilla models ([arXiv 2607.16051](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16051)) |

Two other choices matter more than order. Keeping the first and last layers unshared is best or near-best wherever tested: Middle-Cycle in MoR, Subformer's sandwich, which cuts parameters 25–70% "while retaining performance" ([Subformer](https://github.com/machelreid/subformer)), Huginn's prelude and coda, and Relaxed Recursive Transformers' Gemma result. Sharing attention costs far less than sharing the FFN: in ALBERT at embedding size 768, shared attention scores 81.6 and shared FFN 79.5, against 82.3 unshared ([ALBERT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.11942)), and MixerLoop, which loops only token mixing, keeps about 41.5% of full recurrence's gain at about 45.9% fewer projection FLOPs at 110M ([arXiv 2608.18230](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18230)). In cube terms, A→A→B→B is a "squared generator" move set, like counting half-turns as single moves: it changes how many steps a given function takes, not what is reachable, and the data say the effect is small. Hardware is the better reason to choose it. MobileLLM notes that about 20 MB of on-device SRAM holds roughly one block, so back-to-back repetition avoids reloading weights, and it measured that repeat at +2.6% execution time against +86% for untied depth on an iPhone 13 ([MobileLLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14905)). The user should keep A→B as the cycle unit, because a cycle is what variable pass counts and exits act on; add a unique prelude and coda if the parameter budget allows; and switch to A→A→B→B only on targets where one block fits on-chip. Order alone does not justify a new stage A *[inference]*. If the user wants stage A to search the layout itself, Savarese and Maire's soft sharing, where each layer mixes a bank of templates with learned coefficients, converged to near-strict recurrence in CNNs and could be snapped to the nearest hard layout afterwards; it is untested on transformers ([ICLR 2019](https://openreview.net/forum?id=rJgYxn09Fm)) *[inference]*.

### Kociemba's two lessons: soft phase targets and a stabilizer-preserving refine phase

The student's stage A already behaves like a phase solver. Matching the teacher's internal outputs after cycle k plays the role of "reach G1 after phase 1". Kociemba's first lesson is that phase-optimal is not globally optimal and that searching across the boundary recovers the loss. For distillation, that argues against pinning cycle k to a fixed teacher layer. The mapping can be chosen by dynamic programming, as a monotone shortest path through a student-cycle × teacher-layer CKA similarity matrix, an approach already used to align layers across architectures ([arXiv 2602.19332](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19332)). Checkpoint weights can then be tapered so that stage A ends on output-only KD, letting the student take its own route between checkpoints *[inference]*.

The second lesson is that phase 2 uses a restricted move set that never leaves G1, the stabilizer of what phase 1 solved. The looped analogue is a refine phase that uses a specialized or cheaper block, runs only on tokens or frames that remain unresolved, and leaves resolved ones fixed. Think-at-Hard is the closest validated instance: later iterations activate a LoRA adapter only on tokens the decider marks as hard ([TaH](https://github.com/thu-nics/TaH)). Mixture-of-Recursions shrinks the active token set at each recursion ([MoR](https://github.com/raymin0223/mixture_of_recursions)), and FlashLoop runs Ouro's loops 3–4 on only 25% and 10% of tokens without training ([FlashLoop](https://github.com/Superone77/FlashLoop)). TRM alternates two move types with one network, updating a latent several times and then the answer ([TRM](https://github.com/SamsungSAILMontreal/TinyRecursiveModels)). No paper compares an explicit coarse-then-refine loop with plain A→B→A→B. A concrete variant for the user is two full cycles of A→B on everything, then one cycle of A followed by B plus a refine LoRA on a fixed-capacity top-k of unresolved tokens or patches, with the rest passed through unchanged. Fixed capacity keeps shapes static *[inference]*.

### Per-input path search: large oracle gains, smaller router gains, smaller deployable ones

Searching per-input paths through a frozen LLM's layers, where each layer can be skipped or repeated, reveals large headroom. CoLa's Monte Carlo tree search finds a shorter path that stays correct for over 75% of correctly answered samples, and a path that becomes correct for over 60% of wrong ones ([arXiv 2507.07996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07996)), lifting Llama-3-8B on one math subset from 6.0% to 66.2% with oracle search ([Lacuna summary](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/skip-a-layer-or-loop-it-test-time-depth-adaptation-of-pretrained-llms/wrk_3dc253e95111a84b5bcc40d831bb4944)) *[secondary]*. Distilled routers keep only part of it. Dr.LLM trains 11M router parameters (0.14% of a 3B model) on 4,000 MCTS paths at 50 simulations each in under 4 hours on one A100, and gains **+3.4 points while saving about 5 layers**, losing 0.85% out of domain ([Dr.LLM](https://github.com/parameterlab/dr-llm)). PoLar predicts a whole program up front with about 0.8% overhead ([HF page](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.06574)), but an independent replication found its top-1 prediction "consistently collapsed back to the standard pass". Only its top-k set helped, and "a small number of generic programs are enough to solve most of the questions"; with search, Qwen3-8B rose from 48.6% to 88.8% on the easiest DART-Math level and from 11.2% to 47.2% on the hardest ([arXiv 2609.31360](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.31360)). Learned routing also chooses "how many" far more than "which": per-layer routers over a shared expert pool pick the same expert in both layers of a reuse block only 10.3% of the time, against 5.0% by chance ([MoRE](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18176)).

The user's student changes the economics. Its program space is the set of words over {A, B}. All programs of up to six block applications form a tree of 126 nodes, each one block application, so scoring every program for a calibration input costs about 21× one normal run when prefixes are shared. That is a breadth-first build, exactly how pruning tables are made, and it needs no MCTS *[derived]*. The result is an exact per-input "God's algorithm" on the calibration set: the shortest program that reaches teacher match. Two caveats apply. Programs off the A→B pattern are out of distribution for per-pass norms and FiLM trained on A→B→A→B, so a menu that includes them needs stage-A training on random programs. And the replication suggests the search will mostly rediscover "how many cycles" and "whether to refine", which is the exit head again *[inference]*.

Deployment decides what survives. TensorRT's guide lists "loops, conditionals, and layers requiring data-dependent shapes" among operations CUDA graphs cannot capture ([TensorRT](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/tensorrt/latest/performance/optimization.html)), and TensorRT for RTX graphs "cannot represent runtime-dependent branches" ([TensorRT-RTX](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/tensorrt-rtx/latest/inference-library/work-with-cuda-graphs.html)). The companion research found no evidence of Hexagon, the Neural Engine or the DLA executing a rolled loop, but did find weight deduplication across up to 64 graphs in Qualcomm's QNN ([KBA](https://docs.qualcomm.com/doc/KBA-250306025001/KBA-250306025001_REV_1_QNN_Weight_Sharing_Feature.pdf)) and across functions in Core ML ([coremltools](https://github.com/apple/coremltools/blob/main/docs-guides/source/multifunction-models.md)). The cube's move tables are the right metaphor: precompute a few transitions and look them up.

| Routing form | TensorRT / Jetson | Phone NPU (QNN, Core ML) | Browser (WebGPU) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed layout, unrolled | Yes | Yes | Yes | Deployable |
| Per-sequence or per-frame choice among K precompiled programs sharing weights | One engine or CUDA graph per program, host selects | One graph per program, weights deduplicated | One pipeline set per program | Deployable *[inference]* |
| Per-cycle exit at sequence-step or frame level | Per-cycle subgraphs, one host call each | Same; costly where decode is dispatch-bound | Decide from the previous step to avoid readbacks | Deployable with overhead *[inference]* |
| Per-token depth or routing | Only fixed-capacity top-k shapes | Not demonstrated | Not demonstrated | GPU servers (MoR; TaH2's engine) |
| Arbitrary per-token paths (MoM, CoLa-style) | No | No | No | Not deployed anywhere |

## Gauge symmetry turns layer tying into minutes of assignment problems

### What can be permuted or rotated, and what RoPE forbids

The cube compresses its tables by storing one entry per symmetry class. A transformer has a far larger symmetry group acting on its parameters, and tying layers into a looped block is the problem of choosing, for each teacher layer, the representative of its symmetry class that lies closest to its partners. For multi-head attention with generic weights, the exact gauge group is ((GL(d_k))^h × (GL(d_v))^h) ⋊ S_h: permute the h heads, apply any invertible map to each head's Q/K pair, and any invertible map to each head's V/O pair. That implies more than 1.1 million redundant dimensions in a 110M-parameter Transformer-Base, and valid transforms preserve GPT-2's outputs to about 10⁻⁶ relative error ([NeurIPS 2025 listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/loc/san-diego/136893); [repo](https://github.com/kellywang2030/transformer-gauge-symmetry)). RoPE shrinks the Q/K part to maps that commute with the rotary rotations, "typically a product of copies of GL(1, ℂ)", one 2×2 rotation-scaling per frequency pair ([same listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/loc/san-diego/136893); [arXiv 2607.17146](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17146)).

| Component | Function-preserving freedom | Use when tying layers of one teacher |
|---|---|---|
| Attention heads | Permutation of heads; under GQA, within KV groups plus whole groups *[inference]* | One assignment on an h×h head-similarity matrix |
| Per-head Q/K | Any invertible map without RoPE; with RoPE, only per-frequency rotation-scalings | Restrict to the commutant, or skip |
| Per-head V/O | Any invertible map | Orthogonal Procrustes |
| FFN hidden units | Permutation (gate and up rows with down columns); positive rescaling only for ReLU *[inference]* | Assignment on activation correlations |
| Residual stream | One global orthogonal map under RMSNorm, as SliceGPT and QuaRot use | Not a per-layer freedom; already shared by every layer |
| Norm gains | Fold into the next linear map | Fold before averaging |

The residual row matters. SliceGPT's invariance needs one rotation carried through every layer ([SliceGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15024)), and in permutation merging of BERTs the identity residual permutation gave the lowest barrier, 4.95 against 7.34–9.38 for learned ones ([Verma & Elbayad](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.00986)). Inside one model the residual basis is already common to all layers, so alignment works only on the per-layer freedoms. Attention cannot be aligned matrix by matrix, because logits depend on the composed operator W_Q W_Kᵀ and outputs on W_V W_O, both invariant to the per-head maps. Matching heads on those composed operators, averaging them and refactorizing, as SLOA does sequentially for whole models ([SLOA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06415)), sidesteps the ambiguity *[inference for within-model use]*. Rotation symmetry ([ICML 2025](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/poster/43634)) and generalized linear mode connectivity over permutations, orthogonal and invertible maps ([Theus et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22712)) widen the toolbox further. The evidence that head order matters inside one model is direct: LiSA reports that reusing attention weights across layers "without first rearranging the heads performs poorly" ([LiSA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.01890)).

### Pairwise alignment is cheap; calibration tokens are the binding constraint

Each permutation is one linear assignment problem (LAP) over a similarity matrix, from weights (data-free) or activation correlations. Git Re-Basin's weight matching runs coordinate descent over permutation groups, solving `linear_sum_assignment` until nothing improves ([code](https://github.com/samuela/git-re-basin/blob/main/src/weight_matching.py)); matching all coupled layers jointly is NP-hard by reduction from quadratic assignment ([Crisostomi thesis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01580)). Alignment is what makes averages usable. REPAIR lifts the midpoint of two aligned ResNet50s from under 1% to **56.5%** ImageNet accuracy ([REPAIR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.08403)); CCA Merge reaches 61.41% where direct averaging gets 11.40% ([CCA Merge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05385)); and for transformers, soft optimal-transport alignment beats hard alignment ([Imfeld et al.](https://github.com/graldij/transformer-fusion)). The researchers timed dense LAPs with scipy on a 4-core CPU *[computed]*:

| n (units) | Random matrix | Correlation matrix (matched-unit ρ = 0.6) | Building that correlation matrix from 4,096 tokens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 768 | 0.04 s | 0.01 s | 0.02 s |
| 4,096 | 2.8 s | 0.39 s | 1.3 s |
| 11,008 | 26.1 s | 1.7 s | 9.0 s |

At n = 11,008 the hidden permutation was recovered exactly even at ρ = 0.1 with 4,096 tokens, while at n = 1,024 with ρ = 0.09 and 1,024 tokens pairwise accuracy fell to 35% *[computed]*. Those results fit a simple extreme-value rule: matching is reliable once ρ√T clears √(2 ln n), because the true partner must out-score the best of n noisy competitors whose correlations have spread about 1/√T *[derived]*. The simulated cases sit at 2.9, 3.5 and 4.5 against a threshold of 3.7 for n = 1,024, giving 35%, 65% and 98% accuracy, and the exact recovery at n = 11,008 sits at 6.4 against 4.3. With about twice the threshold token count, T ≈ 4·ln(n)/ρ², a ρ of 0.1 needs about 4,000 tokens and a ρ of 0.05 about 15,000 at n = 11,008. The aligned cross-layer correlations measured in ViTs, about 0.385 for FFN neurons and 0.21 for attention ([Verma et al.](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=t8iuiH46g0)), need only hundreds. Estimation noise is therefore rarely the limit; the limit is how different the layers truly are. The companion quantization research put bulk cross-layer R² at only 0.05–0.15 after alignment, so aligned averaging yields a better starting point for stage A, not a working loop without training.

### Tying many layers needs a cycle-consistent universe, not a chain

Tying three or more teacher layers into one block needs mutually consistent permutations P_1…P_k into a shared basis, so that P_i P_j⁻¹ maps layer j to layer i for every pair. Pairwise permutation methods are measurably inconsistent: "over 50% of the neurons from B don't get matched to the same neuron from C when aligned directly or indirectly through A" ([CCA Merge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05385)). C2M3 maps every model into a universe space with Frank-Wolfe so that every cycle composes to the identity, and reports its best results with REPAIR ([C2M3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17897)); permutation synchronization solves the same problem spectrally ([Pachauri et al.](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2013/poster/3941)). Within one model, the only published tying uses a star: every FFN sublayer is aligned to one anchor ([Verma et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.06126)). The researchers simulated six "layers" of 1,024 units, each a hidden permutation of shared latent units plus independent noise *[computed]*:

| Matched-unit ρ | One pair | Chain 0→1→…→5 | Star to layer 0 | Align-to-mean, leave-one-out | Spectral synchronization |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.09 | 0.348 | 0.109 | 0.361 | **0.643** | 0.362 |
| 0.11 | 0.648 | 0.341 | 0.655 | **0.995** | 0.873 |
| 0.14 | 0.981 | 0.917 | 0.968 | **1.000** | **1.000** |

Chaining compounds errors roughly as p^(k−1) (0.648⁵ ≈ 0.11 against 0.136 observed after five hops), and it never revisits early mistakes. Re-matching each layer to the mean of the other layers turns a 65%-accurate pairwise regime into 99.5% joint accuracy, because k−1 noisy views average out. Including a layer in its own target mean left the solution stuck at the star result, so the mean must leave it out. Spectral synchronization cost 26–30 s against 1.2–2.9 s for align-to-mean, and the comparison favours align-to-mean because the spectral version saw only binary pairwise matches. The data were toy Gaussians, not network activations *[computed]*. The practical recipe is a star to a middle anchor, then one to five rounds of leave-one-out align-to-mean, about k assignments per round, or about 30 s per round for 12 layers at d_ff = 11,008 on a CPU *[derived]*. The gain is largest where per-pair similarity is weak, which is exactly the case for distant layers tied by an A→B→A→B schedule.

### Graph matching and graph cuts decide which layers share a block

Choosing ties is a graph problem on a layer-similarity graph. GeoPair casts pairing as maximum-weight matching on a complete graph of weight matrices, "solved optimally via Edmonds' Blossom algorithm" with a scale-invariant column-space distance, and recovers over 90% of uncompressed accuracy ([GeoPair](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25963)), reportedly 54.09 against 33.11 for Basis Sharing on Llama-3.2-1B at compression ratio 0.2 ([aggregator](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25963)) *[secondary]*. For vision, the Raptor surrogate takes its blocks from a max-cut partition of layer similarities and recovers about **96–98% of DINOv2's linear-probe accuracy with 2–3 recurrent blocks** ([alphaXiv overview](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2512.19941v5)) *[secondary]*, the strongest sign that a per-frame ViT can be rewritten as the user's two-block loop. Blossom gives optimal pairs only, which fits a 2× loop where each shared block ties exactly two layers. With two blocks over many slots, the user's interleaved assignment (alternate slots to A and B) is a constrained 2-colouring, so the useful test is to compare its total within-group aligned similarity against the best contiguous segmentation (dynamic programming) and the best free two-way partition (max-cut or spectral clustering) *[inference]*. Two practical rules recur across these sources: adjacent and middle layers tie best, and first layers, last layers and shallow attention are fragile. Relaxed Recursive Transformers' Gemma did better with first and last preserved, and its unaligned "Average" initialization "performed poorly", possibly because normalization weights were averaged ([RRT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20672)).

### A stage-A initialization recipe

The recipe combines these pieces. Each step has a published precedent, but the combination is untested on a looped student *[inference]*.

| Step | What to do | Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Assign slots | Map teacher layers to A and B slots; score interleaved vs contiguous vs free partitions on aligned similarity; keep first and last layers unique if budget allows | GeoPair; Raptor; MoR Middle-Cycle |
| 2. Fold norms | Fold RMSNorm gains into the following linear maps so they are not averaged | SliceGPT invariance; RRT's norm-averaging failure |
| 3. Collect | Activations and Hessians per layer from ~10K tokens or ~1K frames; check ρ√T against √(2 ln n) | Verma et al. used 10K tokens |
| 4. Align FFNs | Star alignment to a middle anchor, then 1–5 rounds of leave-one-out align-to-mean | Verma et al.; *[computed]* simulation; C2M3 |
| 5. Align attention | Head assignment on composed Q/K and V/O operators; V/O Procrustes; Q/K only within the RoPE commutant | Gauge group; LiSA; SLOA |
| 6. Average | Hessian-weighted mean W* = (Σ WᵢHᵢ)·H_Σ⁻¹, the closed form of summed per-slot gradients; attention via composed operators, then refactorize | Companion quantization research |
| 7. Renormalize and relax | REPAIR-style statistics matching per slot; per-pass norm gains and FiLM from per-slot statistics; per-pass LoRA from truncated SVD of each slot's residual | REPAIR; RRT |
| 8. Choose | Compare starting KD loss against Stepwise and raw Average; keep the best | RRT: Stepwise best for strict ties, Average best with LoRA |

The payoff is unmeasured. Within one model, permutation-aligned FFN tying is "consistently better" than unaligned tying, the gap grows as more sublayers are tied, and over 21% of a ViT's parameters can be removed at 99% of its accuracy with a recovery fine-tune ([Verma et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.06126)). But published within-model ties cover groups of two to four layers, while a two-block student of a 12–36-layer teacher ties 6–18 layers per block. Per-pass relaxation is still needed in that regime, and aligned averaging should be judged by how many stage-A tokens it saves.

## Cube moves cycle forever; a looped block has to contract

### Invertible maps orbit, contractions settle

A cube move sequence is a bijection on a finite set, so every orbit is periodic and nothing ever settles; repetition returns to the start after the element's order. The neural analogue is a reversible block. The additive coupling used by RevNet and Reformer, y₁ = x₁ + F(x₂), y₂ = x₂ + G(y₁), saves activation memory ([RevNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.04585); [Reformer](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04451)), but its Jacobian determinant is exactly 1. The product of the eigenvalue magnitudes is therefore 1, so it cannot have an attracting fixed point; like a cube move it can rotate, shear or cycle, never settle *[derived]*. Invertibility alone does not forbid convergence: an invertible residual block x + g(x) with Lip(g) < 1 can contract in some directions ([i-ResNet](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/behrmann19a/behrmann19a.pdf)). What a looped block needs is contraction toward an input-conditioned fixed point h*(x). If one A→B cycle has Lipschitz constant L < 1, the fixed point is unique, so warm starts cannot land elsewhere; residuals decay geometrically, so early exit is well defined; and ‖h_k − h*‖ ≤ L/(1−L)·‖h_k − h_{k−1}‖ gives a certified distance-to-solution from two measurable quantities *[derived: Banach fixed-point theorem]*. Reversible blocks remain useful for training memory, but the deployed iterated map should not itself be reversible; a reversible solver around a contractive map is the alternative ([RevDEQ](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.12917)).

| Property | Repeated cube word | Healthy looped block | Observed failure modes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Map | Bijection of a finite set | Input-conditioned contraction | Orbits and "sliders" (Huginn), slow drift (Ouro), oscillation (FPRM), collapse (Huginn's first run) |
| Repetition | Returns to start after its order (4 to 1,260) | Residual shrinks geometrically | Accuracy peaks and then falls past trained depth (STARS; Ouro) |
| Fixed points | Only the identity fixes every position | One per input and token | Several, including false solutions (Ren & Liu) |
| Initial state | Never forgotten (invertible) | Forgotten; input kept by re-injection | Path dependence (Anil et al.) |

### Trained looped models mostly converge, cyclically and unevenly

Measured dynamics mostly look like contraction, with exceptions concentrated on the hard tokens. In a 135M depth-recurrent model, the mean divergence between successive loop outputs falls from about **0.39 at loop 2 to 8.5×10⁻⁶ at loop 16**; the median token converges by loop 6, about 10% are still moving at the training-mean depth of 8, whitespace settles first and content words last ([Logan 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14427)). Multi-block loops reach cyclic fixed points: the output of the whole recurrent block stabilizes while each layer settles at its own point, and if a k-block cycle reaches a fixed point, so does every cyclic permutation of its blocks ([Blayney et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.11791)). Their attention patterns lock into a repeating cycle. Huginn and retrofitted Llama converge quickly, while Ouro drifts slowly without reaching a strict fixed point and degrades when run past its trained recurrences. With input injection every norm type except Ouro's reaches a stable fixed point; without it, only pre-norm does ([Blayney et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.11791v1)). Huginn's own plots show most tokens converging, while some use "orbits" and "sliders" for arithmetic and deliberation ([Geiping et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.05171v1)). Answers can stop changing while scores keep moving, and modelling update direction and competitor pairing moves the earliest safe stopping depth 22.5–34.4% earlier ([Luo & Yu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21383)). Loop steps also shrink and grow more orthogonal over training, and an exit rule on the change in step size beats Huginn's KL rule ([Pappone et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23314)). LayerNorm makes the recurrence Jacobian non-normal, so stability follows the spectral margin rather than an operator-norm bound, and some random initializations never reach a fixed point ([Buehlmaier](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.10681)). For the A→B student, the consequence is precise: convergence must be measured on the composite B∘A, comparing states one full cycle apart. Consecutive blocks never stop changing, and that is expected rather than a failure.

### Stabilizers: what further passes must leave alone

The stabilizer of a state x is the set of operations g with g·x = x. In the cube, G1 is the stabilizer of the solved phase-1 coordinates, and phase 2 lives entirely inside it: once orientation and slice membership are solved, no later move disturbs them. A looped student needs the same discipline in soft form. Once a token or patch matches the teacher, further cycles should lie in its stabilizer and leave it essentially unchanged. Training can enforce this with an extra-cycle consistency loss on resolved positions, matching cycle P+1 to cycle P. At run time, gating or sparse updates can freeze resolved positions, as FlashLoop and Think-at-Hard do. The measurable diagnostic is the stabilized fraction s_k, the share of positions whose argmax does not flip and whose cycle residual falls below a tolerance. In a healthy student s_k rises monotonically with k, and a fall is overthinking made visible *[inference]*. Think-at-Hard's oracle gain of up to 7.3% from skipping already-correct tokens prices what this property is worth, and Cube Bench's finding that "unconstrained reflection can induce overthinking" in language models suggests the problem is general ([Cube Bench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.20595)).

### Path independence decides warm starts and extra passes

A looped model is path independent when it reaches the same fixed point or limit cycle from any initial state. The Asymptotic Alignment (AA) score, the cosine similarity between fixed points reached from different initializations, predicts whether extra test-time iterations help. On 13×13 mazes a path-independent network scores AA 1.00 and 100% accuracy against 0.32 and 87.12% for a path-dependent one; on 64-bit prefix sums the figures are 0.99 and 100% against 0.62 and 66.66%. Input injection improves both path independence and accuracy ([Anil et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09961)). Deep Thinking networks without "recall" (re-injecting the input) and a progressive loss fall to 0% on 512-bit prefix sums when run far beyond training. Recall plus a progressive loss, which runs a random number of gradient-free iterations before training the next k, solves 97.12% of 512-bit prefix sums, against 90.27% without the random start ([Bansal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05826); [training code](https://github.com/aks2203/deep-thinking/blob/main/deepthinking/utils/training.py)). Models built this way generalize upward. Equilibrium Reasoners train with at most 16 iterations and still benefit beyond 1,024, with simple cases converging within 1–5 steps ([EqR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21488)), and stochastic loop schedules reduce run-to-run variance across inference loop counts ([Kuo et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29983v1)). States near a fixed point depend less on their path, which a 2026 model exploits for terminal KV sharing: a 3× smaller KV cache matches full-cache training, and a 1.6B looped model runs 12 blocks deep in the memory of 4 ([Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06833)).

Three implications follow for the user's deployments. Early exit should combine the learned head with a convergence guard measured on the B∘A cycle. Warm starts are safe only to the degree the student is path independent. For video they are the big lever: DEQ-Flow solves only frame 0 fully and recycles each frame's fixed point for the next ([DEQ-Flow](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/papers/Bai_Deep_Equilibrium_Optical_Flow_Estimation_CVPR_2022_paper.pdf)), and StreamDEQ runs more than 3× faster ([StreamDEQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.13492)). For token models, warm-starting from the previous token saved only about 1–2 of ~32 steps in Huginn ([Huginn](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05171)), too little to remove a cycle from a three-cycle student. More cycles on harder inputs pay only if the student is trained with random cycle counts that extend past the deployed depth, because a model distilled at one fixed depth is invited to become path dependent, as Ouro's decline past its four trained loops illustrates *[inference]*.

### Levers that buy contraction, and what they cost

| Lever | Measured effect | Cost or risk |
|---|---|---|
| Input re-injection ("recall") | Needed for >97% on 512-bit prefix sums; improves path independence; lets all tested norm types except Ouro's converge | Small adapter |

| Half random, half zero initial states | Promotes path independence (Anil et al. follow-ups) | None |

| Jacobian Frobenius penalty | DEQ-Transformer: ~30 → 12–14 solver steps at perplexity 24.0 → 24.5–24.9; ImageNet: 27–30 → 14–15 steps at 77.5% → 76.8% ([Bai et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.14342); [alphaXiv overview](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2106.14342v1) *[secondary]*) | Hutchinson estimate, applied stochastically ([DEQ repo](https://github.com/locuslab/deq)) |

Every lever trades some expressivity for stability, so teacher-match quality at the deployed depth must be tracked alongside the diagnostics. Published thresholds are scarce: the only concrete one found is Huginn's zero-shot exit at a successive-step KL below 5×10⁻⁴ ([model card](https://huggingface.co/tomg-group-umd/huginn-0125)).

| Diagnostic | Computation | Healthy reading | If not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cycle residual r_k | ‖h after cycle k+1 − h after cycle k‖ / ‖h‖, per token or patch | Geometric decay; the log-slope estimates L | Plateau: run period detection |
| Output change | KL and argmax flips between cycles | KL below ~5×10⁻⁴ (Huginn's zero-shot value, tune per model) | Exit signal and head guard |
| Stabilized fraction s_k | Share of positions with no flip and r_k under tolerance | Rises monotonically | Falls: add extra-cycle consistency loss |

| Period test | ‖h_{k+p} − h_k‖ for p > 1 vs a monotone drift | No orbits | Orbits: damping; drift: train longer depths |
| AA score | Cosine of final states from trained, previous-frame or previous-token, and random starts | ≥ 0.95 *[inference]* | Train with random starts before using warm starts |
| Token correlation | Mean pairwise cosine of states | Well below 1 | Toward 1: collapse, as in Huginn's first run ([Geiping et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.05171)) |
| Quality beyond P | Teacher match at cycles P+1 … 2P | Flat or rising | Falling: path dependence |

## The analogy breaks in ten places, three of them decisive

| Cube property | Looped-model reality | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Finite discrete states and an exactly checkable goal | Continuous states; the goal is teacher match within a tolerance | "Solved" is a threshold choice; labels need a stays-solved definition |
| Every move invertible; the moves form a group | Blocks are not invertible; compositions form a monoid at best | No exact commutators or conjugates; cycling is a failure mode, not a tool |
| Mass concentrated at distance 18–19 of 20 | Required passes concentrated at one, with a heavy tail (93–94% of tokens skip a second pass; median convergence at loop 6 of 8; 94% of ARC-AGI-1 accuracy at step 1) | Exits pay far more for loops than for cubes; the budget should be sized by the tail |
| Search cost exponential in depth, so better heuristics save ~480× | Loop cost linear in passes; a perfect head saves at most P× | Keep the head tiny; spend effort on contraction |
| Free exact labels from random walks backward from the goal | Labels need student and teacher runs | Cheap only because P is small |
| Solving is inverse search back to the identity | A looped model computes forward | Group theory bounds tracking depth, not distillation quality |
| Symmetries of states (48) compress tables | The exploitable symmetries are of parameters (gauge) | Use them for initialization; input symmetries such as flips are speculative |
| Generators fixed and known | Blocks learned, changing during training | Order and phase choices interact with training; judge them after training |
| Phase targets are exact invariants | Teacher checkpoints are soft, and the student may route differently | Over-pinned checkpoints cost quality, which is Kociemba's lesson in reverse |
| Search width buys quality (beams of 2²⁰) | One trajectory per input on device | Multi-trajectory gains, such as Probabilistic TRM's 87.3% → 99.06% pass@K on Sudoku ([arXiv 2605.19943](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19943)), belong offline or on servers |

Three of these breaks are decisive. The difficulty distribution is the reverse of the cube's: most inputs are easy, so adaptive depth is worth more for models than for cubes. Linear rather than exponential cost caps what any exit head can save, so the head should be cheap rather than accurate at all costs. And non-invertibility turns the cube's engine into the loop's failure mode, so the dynamics section, not the heuristics, decides whether exits, warm starts and extra passes are safe. The cube's 48 symmetries also differ in kind from the gauge symmetries used here. The analogy is tightest for the training pipeline (labels, initialization, phase targets) and loosest for runtime behaviour.

## A concrete design for the two-stage pipeline

The design keeps the user's A→B→A→B→A→B student and two-stage distillation, and adds only what the cube-derived techniques need. A "cycle" below is one A→B pair, so the student runs three cycles, six block applications. The design as a whole is untested *[inference]*. The companion research's device conclusions still hold: unrolled weight-shared graphs, sequence- or frame-level exits, and per-pass norms and FiLM that fold into scale vectors at no runtime cost, whereas per-pass LoRA is a decode tax, at 21.0 vs 33.4 tok/s unmerged vs merged at batch 1 on an H100 ([CoLT-Drive](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.00242)).

|---|---|---|---|

| Stage 0 (initialization) | The eight-step recipe: graph-scored slot assignment, folded norms, star plus leave-one-out align-to-mean for FFNs, head and V/O alignment, Hessian-weighted averaging, REPAIR-style renormalization, SVD-initialized per-pass parameters | Symmetry quotient; cycle-consistent universe; matching and cuts | Verma et al., LiSA, C2M3, RRT, *[computed]* simulation |
| Stage A (internal matching) | Per-cycle teacher checkpoints with a DP-chosen monotone mapping, tapered toward output KD; a readout loss after every cycle; random cycle counts from 1 to P+1 with a gradient-free prefix; half the batch from random initial states (and, for video, previous-frame states with noise); extra-cycle consistency on resolved positions; a stochastic Jacobian or spectral penalty; the KV policy (last-cycle or ragged) fixed in the attention masks | Subgroup chain; stabilizer; contraction | Bansal et al., Huginn, STARS, Bai et al., Ouro |

## Conclusion

The cube's most transferable lesson is about where the intelligence sits, not about its moves. Every strong solver puts it in a cheap estimate of distance-to-goal plus a search budget. For a looped model the search budget is the pass count, and because that cost is linear rather than exponential, the estimate should stay tiny and err toward running longer. What makes "running longer" safe is contraction, which the cube cannot teach because its moves are invertible. The two halves meet: the dynamics work in stage A is what licenses the heuristic work in stage B, and an exit head on a student that overthinks inherits a safety guarantee that does not exist.

Two group-theoretic ideas do more work than the cube metaphor suggests. The stabilizer view turns Kociemba's restricted phase-2 move set into a concrete training rule and diagnostic: later cycles should fix what earlier cycles solved, and the share of positions they leave fixed should only grow. The quotient view turns layer tying into choosing canonical representatives of gauge orbits, a problem that is cheap (seconds per assignment), well tooled (Hungarian, optimal transport, cycle-consistent universes, Blossom matching) and almost unused for initializing looped students. Neither has been tested on a looped student, and both can be measured in days. The audit stage costs no training and tells the user which of the cube's lessons their student actually needs.
