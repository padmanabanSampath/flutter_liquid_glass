# Speculate in decode, skip work everywhere else

**A looped model gets faster through different levers depending on what limits the device, and the ranking flips between regimes.** In batch-1 token decode, which waits on weight bytes and dispatch overhead, the loop itself becomes an asset: every pass uses the same block, so work at several depths can share one weight read. Self-speculative decoding with a one-pass draft exploits that without retraining and is measured at **2.42× on Ouro-2.6B and 3.5–7× on 32-loop Huginn-class models**, though only on datacenter GPUs; a three-pass student has a ceiling near 3×, and an output head read once per draft can erase most of it. Cross-loop parallel architectures go further (two loops at **1.02× the latency of one**) but need re-distillation, while early exit saves only 20–40% of passes and saves nothing until the KV cache is redesigned. In prefill and per-frame vision, which wait on arithmetic, only removing work counts: activation quantization where the silicon has a faster path (NVFP4 prefill **3–6.3×** over 4-bit weight-only on Jetson Thor, W4A8 **4.3×** over BF16 on a phone CPU, int16 activations **1.8×** on Hexagon, W8A8 **1.3×** for a ViT on the A17 Pro Neural Engine, but about **1.0×** for INT8 ViTs on Orin), 20–40% fewer passes, and token reduction that survives on-device overheads. For video streams, reusing the previous frame's computation (2–4× fewer FLOPs, 1.5–2.3× measured on GPUs) is the largest single lever. Most levers enter the user's pipeline cheaply: per-pass supervision, random pass counts and a fixed KV policy in stage A, exit or draft heads in stage B, and all-pass calibration afterwards. The largest architectural wins need a new stage A. A full stack plausibly reaches about **1.5–3× on decode and per-frame vision, and more on prefill where the hardware has a fast low-precision path** *[inference]*, but no loop-specific technique has been timed on a phone, browser or Jetson, and many figures come from search excerpts, READMEs and a GitHub catalog because paper sites were blocked. A week of on-device profiling should come first, because in small models with large vocabularies the output head and KV cache, not the loop, can dominate decode.

## Paper sites were blocked and no loop trick has been timed on a target device

The five research notes behind this report were compiled on 2026-10-12 in an environment whose proxy blocked arXiv, OpenReview, Hugging Face, ACL Anthology, nvidia.com, developer.chrome.com and the Apple and Qualcomm sites. Paper figures therefore come from search-engine excerpts of those pages. Runtime and device figures mostly come from primary GitHub material read directly: READMEs, pull requests, issues, and benchmark tables and vendor documentation kept in repositories such as TensorRT Edge-LLM, Qualcomm AI Hub, coremltools, ExecuTorch and ONNX Runtime. Many 2026 looped-model papers are known only from one-line summaries in the Awesome-Loop-Models catalog, a list regenerated daily on GitHub ([catalog](https://github.com/huskydoge/Awesome-Loop-Models)), and nearly all of them are unreviewed preprints.

A claim with a source link and no tag is a published or released result as excerpted. *[secondary]* marks a figure seen only in a third-party summary or a catalog summary, *[derived]* marks arithmetic by the research team or this report, and *[inference]* marks a judgment that no source has tested. Speed figures also state their kind: **measured** means wall-clock time on named hardware, **FLOP-only** means a counted or analytical saving, **simulated** means modelled timings such as oracle exits, and **vendor claim** means the hardware or runtime owner's own figure.

The most important caveat concerns hardware. Every measured loop-specific speedup in the notes comes from datacenter or workstation GPUs (RTX A6000, B200/B300, H100-class, RTX 3090). The only on-device measurement involving shared weights is MobileLLM's iPhone 13 result that running a block twice back to back adds **2.6% execution time, against 86%** for an untied model of the same depth ([MobileLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14905v2)). That prices an extra pass; it does not test a technique for removing one. Every phone, browser and Jetson number below is for a non-looped model, so every device-level speedup claimed here for a looped model is an inference that the plan at the end must test. ## Bytes bind decode, arithmetic binds prefill and frames, dispatches bind small models

A looped model runs K unique blocks for N passes, so the user's A→B→A→B→A→B student executes six block-passes per token or frame. Relative to one pass, that multiplies four things: arithmetic, weight reads (unless a block is still cached when it runs again), KV caches (one per block-pass) and kernel dispatches. A pre-norm block with multi-head attention and a SwiGLU feed-forward network costs about **24d² linear FLOPs per token per pass**, two-thirds of it in the FFN, plus about 4dL for attention over L cached tokens; at d = 2048 and L = 4096, attention is about a quarter of a pass *[derived]*. Which multiplier hurts depends on the regime. Batch-1 decode multiplies vectors by matrices, so it waits on memory: an iPhone 16 Pro decodes a 1B 4-bit model at 40–50 tok/s, consistent with a bandwidth limit ([benchmarks](https://www.ertas.ai/blog/llm-run-on-iphone-benchmarks)) *[secondary]*, and a STREAM test on an AGX Orin reached only 54.5 of its 204.8 GB/s ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/jetson-agx-orin-32gb-measured-memory-bandwidth-much-lower-than-theoretical-spec/331766)). Prefill and per-frame encoders push hundreds or thousands of tokens through each matrix, so they wait on arithmetic, and weight sharing leaves their cost untouched: ALBERT-xxlarge has 70% of BERT-large's parameters yet trains about 3× slower ([ALBERT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.11942)). For scale, a 2B VLM's FP16 vision encoder takes 11.7 ms per ~265-token image on Thor, 36.2 ms on AGX Orin and 141.3 ms on Orin Nano ([Edge-LLM](https://github.com/NVIDIA/TensorRT-Edge-LLM/blob/main/docs/source/user_guide/performance/performance-benchmarks.md)); a three-pass looped encoder costs what a three-times-deeper one does. Small models on browsers and NPUs add a third regime in which fixed per-operation costs dominate. WebGPU dispatches cost 24–36 µs on Vulkan and 32–71 µs on Metal ([arXiv 2604.02344](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02344v1)), so a two-block, three-pass model at about 15 dispatches per block spends **2–6 ms per token on dispatch alone** *[derived]*. MediaTek's NPU decodes Gemma 3 1B at a dispatch-bound ~16 tok/s ([LiteRT community](https://huggingface.co/litert-community/Gemma3-1B-IT-Tensor-NPU)), and eager looped speculation on a B200 issued about 1,400 kernels per pass while the GPU sat 13–16% busy ([vllm-rlt #43](https://github.com/ThinkFlowLab/vllm-rlt/issues/43)).

The regime decides whether looping is a liability or an asset. In a byte-bound pass, extra rows in a matrix multiply cost almost nothing until the multiply becomes compute-bound. An untied model cannot exploit that across depth, because a token at layer 3 and another at layer 9 use different matrices. In a looped model every depth uses the same block, so **work from different depths can share one weight read**. Every loop-specific execution trick with a measured gain exploits exactly this: self-speculative decoding verifies drafts at several depths in one call, cross-loop parallelism runs loop 2 of one token alongside loop 1 of the next, depth-wise batching packs requests at different depths, and back-to-back repetition keeps a block in cache. In compute-bound prefill and vision the spare rows do not exist, so the same tricks add arithmetic rather than removing it *[inference]*.

Two quantities that have nothing to do with the loop decide how much any loop lever can save in decode. The output head is read once per token and again for every draft or logit-based exit check, and the KV cache grows with passes times context. An illustrative student makes the point *[derived]*:

| Per decoded token: d = 2048, int4 weights, 128K vocabulary, fp16 GQA KV (8 heads × 64) | 2 unique blocks × 3 passes | 8 unique blocks × 3 passes |
|---|---|---|
| Block weights read | ~150 MB | ~600 MB |
| Output head read | ~131 MB | ~131 MB |
| KV read at 4K / 16K context | ~48 / ~192 MB | ~192 / ~768 MB |
| Bytes saved by skipping the last pass, at 4K | ~20% | ~29% |

In the two-block case the head is nearly half the weight traffic, and at 16K context the KV reads exceed all block weights, so on long contexts the KV policy matters more than the pass count. Caches cannot rescue either case. One d = 2048 block is about 25 MB at int4, larger than Orin's 4 MB GPU L2 ([Hot Chips](https://hc34.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/ADAS%20and%20Grace/HC2022.NVIDIA.Mike_Ditty.v6.pdf)), the Snapdragon 8 Elite's 8 MB system cache ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Qualcomm-Snapdragon-8-Elite-Processor-Benchmarks-and-Specs.908499.0.html)) and the A18 Pro's 24 MB ([heise](https://heise.de/-9960558)); a d = 768 block is about 3.5 MB, so an A+B pair fits comfortably only in the iPhone's cache *[derived]*.

| Regime | Binding resource | Lever families that can pay | Lever families that cannot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Batch-1 decode | Weight, head and KV bytes; then dispatches | Multi-depth batching (speculation, cross-loop parallelism); fewer passes with a KV redesign; fewer bytes (4-bit weights, activation sparsity, KV sharing); fusion | Activation-precision cuts (except on Hexagon); token pruning inside a step |
| LLM prefill | Arithmetic; attention for long prompts | Activation quantization on fast-math units; token-sparse late passes; exits; cheaper attention | Speculation, depth batching, residency |
| Per-frame vision | Arithmetic at 196–1,000+ tokens; dispatches for tiny models | Fewer passes; fewer tokens; activation quantization; linear attention at high resolution | Speculation; residency except for tiny low-resolution models |
| Video stream | Arithmetic per frame, with heavy redundancy between frames | Temporal reuse; warm start; frame-level exits; then per-frame levers | As per-frame |

Arithmetic sits under every row. Relative to 16-bit weights, 4-bit weights are the foundation of decode speed: on a OnePlus 12 CPU, ExecuTorch's W4A8 Llama-3.2-1B decodes at **50.2 vs 19.2 tok/s** for BF16 ([ExecuTorch](https://github.com/pytorch/executorch/blob/main/examples/models/llama/README.md)). ### Self-speculation is the strongest lever that needs no retraining

In 2025–2026 a cluster of training-free, lossless methods turned a looped model's early passes into its own draft model. WaveFront Decoding drafts from an early recurrence and processes token states at different positions and depths in one batched call, reaching **2.42× on Ouro-2.6B (draft at 1 of 4 loops), 3.54× on Huginn-3.5B, and 4.81× when Huginn shares KV across recurrences**, measured on one RTX A6000 at batch 1 with GSM8K and MATH-500 accuracy comparable to ordinary decoding ([WFD](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.23033)). LoopSpec starts computing the token after a draft before the draft is verified, reporting up to 3.36× on Ouro and up to 6.83× on an unnamed looped model ([LoopSpec](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.17184)). Depth-asynchronous self-speculation reports 4.00–6.96× mean throughput across four recurrent checkpoints, helped by letting shallow drafts read full-depth values, which raised draft agreement on Huginn from 47.2% to 66.9% ([DAS](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.34538v1)), and a diffusion-forcing sampler lifted Huginn's GSM8K throughput from **36.1 to 182.2 tok/s** at about 1% accuracy cost ([parallel samplers](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.14961)). The one serving-engine implementation, vllm-rlt's synchronous scheme drafting at 2 of Ouro-1.4B's 4 loops, measured **1.33×, 1.51×, 1.66× and 1.80×** with 1, 2, 4 and 8 drafts on a B300 at concurrency 1, almost exactly its all-accepted cost model 2(K+1)/(K+2) ([vllm-rlt PR #44](https://github.com/ThinkFlowLab/vllm-rlt/pull/44)). Since 29 tok/s for a 1.4B model on a B300 is far below the bandwidth limit, part of that gain came from amortizing kernel launches over more tokens *[inference]*, which is also what edge runtimes need.

The user's student has the least headroom of any model in these papers. A scheme that drafts after d of D passes is capped near D/d: about 3× for a three-pass student drafting after one pass and 1.5× drafting after two, against 4× for Ouro and 8× or more for Huginn *[derived]*. A synchronous round of four drafts reaches about 2.1× even with every draft accepted, so the pipelined schedules of WaveFront and LoopSpec are needed to approach the cap *[derived]*. The output head lowers the cap further when each draft must read it. In the two-block example above, a draft after pass 1 still reads about 181 MB per token, so a round of four drafts plus one full verification step reads about 1,005 MB and **breaks even only if about 3.6 of the 5 possible tokens are accepted, peaking near 1.4×**. A draft head covering an eighth of the vocabulary moves break-even to about 2 accepted tokens and the peak to about 2.6×; with eight unique blocks, full-head drafting peaks near 1.8× and the small head near 2.3× *[derived]*. A cheap draft head, either a truncated vocabulary or a low-rank projection fitted in stage B, is therefore part of the lever for small students with large vocabularies *[inference]*.

The student also needs a pass-1 readout worth drafting from. Ouro, trained with an exit loss at every loop, scores 51.6 MMLU after one loop against 74.6 after four ([Ouro](https://ouro-llm.github.io/)), and WaveFront still drafted from loop 1. A student distilled only at its final depth has never been asked to make pass 1 predictive, so stage A should add a readout loss after each pass. On devices, verification suits static graphs, because K+1 positions is a fixed shape that an NPU graph or a CUDA Graph can hold *[inference]*. In ONNX Runtime's WebGPU backend, verifying four or more positions also crosses the threshold at which int4 matmuls switch from dequantize-to-float kernels to the DP4A int8 path on non-Apple GPUs ([ORT](https://github.com/microsoft/onnxruntime/blob/main/onnxruntime/contrib_ops/webgpu/quantization/dp4a_matmul_nbits.cc)), which changes speed and numerics and must be measured *[inference]*. No on-device runtime supports looped self-speculation today: llama.cpp runs looped models by unrolling passes over shared weights with a KV slot per pass, and its open Ouro port decodes about 4× slower than a single pass with no early exit ([llama.cpp PR #29823](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29823)).

Cross-loop parallelism exploits the same spare rows by design rather than by speculation. The Parallel Loop Transformer computes different loops for different tokens in one forward pass, shares the first loop's KV with later loops and adds gated sliding-window attention. On an in-house MoE with 680M active parameters at batch 4, its two-loop model decodes at **4.9 ms per token, against 9.4 ms for ordinary two-loop execution and 4.8 ms with no loop**, with the same 39.7 average score as the sequential loop and a KV cache of 284M against 560M ([PLT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.24824)). It drafts nothing, so the output head does not cap it, but it needs a new architecture distilled from scratch, it has been reported for two loops only, and a code-model follow-up found that two loops deliver most of the gain ([LoopCoder-v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18023)) *[secondary]*. StagFormer's staggered design, which includes a weight-shared variant, claims a 33% decode saving that is **simulated** ([StagFormer](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.15665)). Cross-loop parallelism and self-speculation compete for the same idle arithmetic, so a deployment should choose one *[inference]*.

### Exits save passes only after the KV cache is redesigned

Early exit has a structural problem in autoregressive models: a token that stops after pass 1 leaves no pass-2 or pass-3 keys and values for the tokens that follow. Ouro ships a trained exit gate, yet its released code runs all four loops for every token for precisely this reason, so early exit saves nothing as shipped ([arXiv 2610.09013](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09013)) *[secondary]*. The fixes all decide which KV a later token reads. Ragged KV has later tokens read each earlier token's exit-depth entries and cuts **FLOPs and KV memory by up to 30%** at full-depth quality on Ouro, a FLOP-only figure ([arXiv 2610.09013](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09013)) *[secondary]*. Mixture-of-Recursions stores KV only for tokens active at each recursion, bringing memory and cache traffic to about (N_r+1)/(2N_r) of a vanilla model ([MoR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10524)). "Shared Memory in Looped Transformers" pretrains models in which only the first recursion writes KV, with 76–79% less context memory and lower perplexity than a same-size transformer ([arXiv 2610.02383](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02383)) *[secondary]*. Huginn's cache keeps entries per step and position with lookup strategies such as "latest available step", and its code warns that adaptive compute must be benchmarked at batch 1 because batched caches leak deeper KV from other sequences and make exits look better than they are ([Huginn code](https://github.com/seal-rg/recurrent-pretraining/blob/main/recpre/raven_modeling_minimal.py)). Even with KV solved, standard-transformer exits have realized little: a 2024 framework for CALM-style exits measured only **1.25× over vLLM** ([arXiv 2407.20272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20272)).

How many passes can go is the second question. Ouro-1.4B's MMLU at one to four loops is **51.6, 67.6, 73.6 and 74.6**, and its trained gate reaches about 66% at 2.5 average loops against 67.35% at four, a figure that conflicts with the per-depth table and probably reflects a different setup ([Ouro](https://ouro-llm.github.io/)). A training-free convergence exit matched depth-8 quality at **4.94 average loops (−38%)** on a 135M model trained with random depth, but that is a FLOP proxy from a teacher-forced harness, and a linear router trained on the same labels saved nothing ([arXiv 2607.14427](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14427); [Pith review](https://pith.science/paper/2607.14427)) *[secondary]*. *(Correction, 2026-10-13: an earlier version cited arXiv 2608.18222 as counter-evidence that Huginn-3.5B's per-token states never settle. That paper could not be located, and Blayney et al. report that Huginn reaches a fixed point quickly ([arXiv 2604.11791](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.11791v1)), so the claim is withdrawn. Weaker evidence of incomplete settling remains: Ouro drifts without a strict fixed point, and about 10% of tokens are still updating at the trained depth; see the graph-theory report.)* Trained token-level depth does better when the model is built for it: Think-at-Hard skips **94% of second iterations** while beating fixed two-iteration baselines ([TaH](https://github.com/thu-nics/TaH)), and T-LoopFormer matches an untied 24-layer model's perplexity at **0.197 vs 0.202 ms per token** on a datacenter GPU ([arXiv 2609.15160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15160)) *[secondary]*. With only three passes the granularity is coarse, so the user should expect **2.1–2.4 average passes at under 1% loss, roughly 1.15–1.35× real** once exit checks and KV handling are paid for *[inference]*.

Two details decide whether that estimate holds. First, the exit check must not read the output head: in the two-block example a logit-based check after each pass reads more bytes than the pass it might save, so the criterion should be a hidden-state signal, such as the relative state change or cosine thresholds in Huginn's code ([Huginn code](https://github.com/seal-rg/recurrent-pretraining/blob/main/recpre/raven_modeling_minimal.py)), or a tiny trained gate *[derived]*. Second, learned halting is fragile. ACT's author called its ponder-cost weight one to which "the behaviour of the network is quite sensitive" ([ACT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.08983)); PonderNet's probabilistic halting used about 6× fewer steps than UT+ACT on bAbI ([PonderNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.05407)); and a 2026 diagnosis on Ouro found that fixed-prior depth supervision shapes better stop signals than jointly learned gates ([arXiv 2607.20519](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20519)) *[secondary]*. With two possible exit points, a per-pass classifier trained after the backbone is enough.

Static truncation is the simplest exit and needs no KV redesign. Running the student at two passes instead of three saves a third of the work and keeps graphs static, but Ouro loses about 1 MMLU point dropping from four loops to three and 7 dropping to two, so a student never trained at shorter depth should not be truncated blind. Loop-count training makes shorter runs usable. LoopFormer conditions each loop on time and step size and trains with shortcut consistency so one checkpoint serves several budgets ([LoopFormer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11451)); Elastic Looped Transformers distill the full-depth output into randomly sampled intermediate exits ([ELT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09168)); and loop-wise contrastive decoding lets Huginn at 16 of 32 iterations beat its own full-depth baseline ([LoopCD](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02185v1)), summarized elsewhere as cutting forward FLOPs 22.5–48.2% ([papers.cool](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2610.02185)) *[secondary]*. Warm-starting a token's loop from the previous token's latent saves only about 1–2 of ~32 Huginn steps ([Huginn](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05171)), so it will not remove a whole pass from a three-pass student.

### Bytes, heads and dispatches set the floor under every decode trick

Dispatch overhead is the cheapest large win in browsers. Fusing operations so that a WebGPU decode step issued 564 instead of 876 dispatches raised throughput **53% on Vulkan** and did nothing on CUDA ([arXiv 2604.02344](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02344v1)), and one ONNX Runtime fusion gained 5.2% on a 0.8B model but 1.1% on 4B, because fixed costs weigh more on small models ([ORT commit](https://app.semanticdiff.com/gh/microsoft/onnxruntime/commit/a49da870aaaeded4b7d8bd9e937fedb2cf321493)). CUDA Graphs gave **1.259× on an H100 but 1.028× on an L4**, whose slower memory hides launch costs ([arXiv 2605.30571](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30571v1)); Orin's bandwidth puts it at the L4 end *[inference]*. Looping does not reduce the number of dispatches, but it makes every pass identical, so pipelines and bind groups can be created once, and one pass can be captured as a CUDA Graph or encoded once and replayed for each pass *[inference]*.

Activation sparsity is the most edge-proven way to shrink a pass's bytes. TEAL, which needs no training, measured **1.53× single-batch decoding at 40% sparsity and 1.8× at 50%** on 7B–70B models ([TEAL](https://github.com/FasterDecoding/TEAL)); CATS measured about 15% with a custom kernel ([CATS](https://github.com/ScalingIntelligence/CATS)); and PowerInfer-2 runs a ReLU-fied 47B MoE at **11.68 tok/s on a OnePlus 12** ([PowerInfer-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06282v2)). Two loop-specific facts shape its use. No source has measured whether sparsity masks are stable across passes, but in looped MoE models **25–53% of tokens reach completely different experts on pass 2** than on pass 1 ([arXiv 2605.09165](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.09165)), so thresholds should be calibrated per block and per pass *[inference]*. And the 80–90% sparsity of ReLU-fied models costs real training when retrofitted, under 150B tokens for TurboSparse ([TurboSparse](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05955v1)), whereas a student whose FFN uses ReLU from the start of stage A pays nothing extra, at the risk of matching a SwiGLU teacher's internals less well *[inference]*. The kernels exist for CPUs and CUDA, not for WebGPU or phone NPUs, and TEAL's are FP16-only.

KV policy decides long-context decode. Ouro reports that keeping only the last loop's KV during decode holds GSM8K at **78.85 vs 78.92 with 4× less KV memory**, that keeping only the first loop's collapses it to 18.73, and that sharing caches across loops during prefill hurts accuracy ([Ouro](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25741)). For the user's student, keeping only the last pass's KV per block position would cut KV three-fold *[derived]*, and whether that works without retraining is a one-day test. Two cautions apply. MoR's first-recursion sharing cuts memory to 1/N_r but leaves cache traffic at the full level ([MoR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10524)). And the large decode gains reported for compressed loop KV, such as Hybrid Latent Attention's 10.7× smaller cache and **2.5× faster decoding at 1K context and 7.4× at 16K** on Ouro ([arXiv 2610.07940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07940)) *[secondary]*, or FlashLoop's 6× smaller cache from int4 residuals between adjacent loops ([FlashLoop](https://github.com/Superone77/FlashLoop)), come from datacenter GPUs and long contexts.

The remaining decode levers matter less on edge devices. Residency is the only lever in which the loop itself saves bytes, but it works only for small blocks. MobileLLM's measured +2.6% applies to a block repeated back to back, whereas the user's A→B→A→B order needs A and B cached together, which on these devices means widths of about 768 or less at int4 *[derived]*; A→A→B→B order is a different function and needs a new stage A. Jetson exposes persisting-L2 controls without the MIG restriction that disables them on datacenter GPUs ([CUDA guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-programming-guide/04-special-topics/l2-cache-control.html)), but nobody has measured them on looped weights. MoE inside the loop cuts per-pass FFN bytes to roughly k/E, and looped MoE models scale where dense looped models do not: MoEUT roughly halves FFN multiply-accumulates ([MoEUT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16039)) and SwitchHead matches dense perplexity at 44% of the compute ([SwitchHead](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.07987)). But MoE spends back the memory that looping saved, divergent routing touches more distinct experts per token, and no looped MoE has been timed on any edge device. Depth-wise batching has nothing to batch for one user; its measured gains, 1.5–1.9× offline throughput from Continuous Depth Batching ([CDB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09444)) and up to 2.06× for MoR at maximum batch ([MoR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10524)), are serving results, and Relaxed Recursive Transformers' 2–3× is an oracle-exit simulation ([RRT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20672)). Finally, per-pass LoRA is a decode tax: an unmerged rank-48 LoRA decoded at **21.0 vs 33.4 tok/s** merged at batch 1 on an H100 ([CoLT-Drive](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.00242)), whereas per-pass norms and FiLM gains fold into scale vectors at no cost *[inference]*.

### Activation quantization pays only where the silicon has a faster path

Weight-only quantization cuts bytes and leaves the math in floating point, so it barely touches compute-bound work. Only quantizing activations moves multiplies onto faster integer or low-precision units, and only where those units exist. On Jetson Thor, NVFP4 (4-bit weights and activations) prefills **3.1× faster than INT4 AWQ on Qwen3-0.6B and 6.3× faster on Qwen3-8B** while decode is unchanged, and FP8 prefills gemma-4-31B 2.8× faster than AWQ ([Edge-LLM](https://github.com/NVIDIA/TensorRT-Edge-LLM/blob/main/docs/source/user_guide/performance/performance-benchmarks.md)); the smaller the model, the smaller the gain, which matters for edge students. Orin has no FP8 or FP4 engines, and every published Orin LLM row is INT4 AWQ weight-only ([support matrix](https://github.com/NVIDIA/TensorRT-Edge-LLM/blob/main/docs/source/user_guide/getting_started/support-matrix.md)), so an INT8 prefill gain on Orin is unmeasured. On a OnePlus 12 CPU, W4A8 with dynamic int8 activations prefills Llama-3.2-1B at **260.5 vs 60.3 tok/s** for BF16 ([ExecuTorch](https://github.com/pytorch/executorch/blob/main/examples/models/llama/README.md)). On a Galaxy S25 NPU, int16 activations prefill Llama-3.2-3B at **728 vs 396 tok/s** for fp16 ([AI Hub](https://github.com/quic/ai-hub-models/blob/main/src/qai_hub_models/models/llama_v3_2_3b_instruct/perf.yaml)), and the NPU prefills Qwen3-0.6B at 2,783 tok/s against 504 on the GPU ([AI Hub](https://github.com/quic/ai-hub-models/blob/main/src/qai_hub_models/models/qwen3_0_6b/perf.yaml)). In Chrome, ONNX Runtime uses packed int8 dot products only when activations are quantized, at least four rows are multiplied and the GPU is not Apple's, because "Metal … does not appear to have native dp4a support" ([ORT](https://github.com/microsoft/onnxruntime/blob/main/onnxruntime/contrib_ops/webgpu/quantization/dp4a_matmul_nbits.cc)); Google's figure of 1.6–2.9× for int8 over f16 is a vendor claim ([Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/io24-webassembly-webgpu-2)). WebNN, the browser's route to NPUs, is still in origin trial ([blink-dev](https://groups.google.com/a/chromium.org/g/blink-dev/c/5CWKSChYo98/m/xMw0U5NkAAAJ)).

ViTs are less forgiving than LLM prefill. On an iPhone 15 Pro, W8A8 cut MobileViTv2 from **1.36 to 1.03 ms (1.32×)** and ResNet50 by 1.79×, while 8-bit weight-only made ResNet50 slower ([coremltools](https://github.com/apple/coremltools/blob/main/docs-guides/source/opt-quantization-perf.md)); Apple recommends activation quantization only for models running mostly on the Neural Engine, whose faster int8 path arrived with the A17 Pro and M4 ([coremltools](https://github.com/apple/coremltools/blob/main/docs-guides/source/opt-overview.md)). On a Galaxy S25, ViT-B/16 ran in **3.16 ms at w8a16 against 4.42 ms at fp16, but 5.81 ms at w8a8** ([AI Hub](https://github.com/quic/ai-hub-models/blob/main/src/qai_hub_models/models/vit/perf.yaml)). On Orin Nano, ModelOpt INT8 gained about 1% on an isolated ViT-S and made a ViT-S+DPT encoder 2.7× slower ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/tensorrt-model-optimizer-int8-quantization-causes-2-7x-performance-regression-on-jetson-orin-nano-4gb-vit-s-dpt-architecture/357835)) *[secondary]*. On Thor, INT8 was no faster than FP16 on RT-DETR and Swin-UNet ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/jetson-thor-int8-quantization-show-no-performance-gain-over-fp16/353308)) *[secondary]*, and FP8 gained about 20% on a base ViT because attention is bound by softmax, which runs in FP32 ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/low-vit-performance-gain-on-jetson-thor-using-fp8-vs-fp16/349329)) *[secondary]*.

Looping makes low activation precision riskier, because each pass re-applies the same quantized block to a state that already carries the previous pass's error. Calibrating GPTQ on first-pass activations alone was worse than round-to-nearest on 5 of 9 looped checkpoints, while accumulating the Hessian across all passes beat both on all 9 and recovered bf16-level accuracy on Huginn ([arXiv 2609.30820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30820)). LoopQ keeps one quantized backbone and adds loop-dependent activation scales and adapters touching under 0.12% of parameters, improving W4A4 accuracy by 68.8% over the best static baseline ([LoopQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16343)). Loopy uses per-token dynamic scales and finds that configuration rankings change with recurrent depth, so it selects them at the deployed depth ([Loopy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05265v1)). In weight-tied recursive reasoners, per-tensor 4-bit activations took Sudoku accuracy **from 84.1% to 0%**, while per-block MXInt4 restored 80.1% ([arXiv 2607.16237](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16237)). All of this is simulated quantization with no device latency. The practical rule is 8-bit or int16 activations by default and 4-bit activations only with block scales, which among the targets only Thor supports natively *[inference]*. Per-pass static scales cost O(passes × layers) scalars and nothing at run time on GPUs and CPUs, but static-graph NPUs fix activation encodings at compile time, so per-pass scales require an unrolled graph. Per-token dynamic scales fit GPUs and CPUs, since ONNX Runtime's WebGPU path computes per-block activation scales on the fly and XNNPACK quantizes activations dynamically, but they do not fit NPUs *[inference]*.

### Fewer passes and fewer tokens are the device-independent levers

For prefill, FlashLoop is the only measured loop-specific work-remover. It observes that the tokens still changing in later loops form a shrinking subset of those that changed before, so it runs Ouro's loops 1–2 densely and updates only **25% and 10% of tokens in loops 3–4**. It reports 8K-token prefill on Ouro-2.6B falling from 214.4 to 121.8 TFLOPs, up to 1.64× end to end and a cache shrinking from 16.9 to 6.9 GiB, all without training ([FlashLoop](https://github.com/Superone77/FlashLoop)); its SGLang integration is a draft whose tests were failing ([SGLang PR](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/41485)). Applied to the user's student, sparse updates on the last two of six block-passes would leave about 72.5% of token-pass work, and keep rates of 1.0, 0.5 and 0.25 across the three passes about 58% *[derived]*. Exits with ragged KV save up to 30% of prefill FLOPs. Skipping attention on some passes is the cheap structural cut: dropping 4 of Mistral-7B's 32 attention layers gave 1.22× at a 0.4–0.7 point loss and dropping 8 gave 1.45× at 1.5–2.4 points, with no fine-tuning ([LLM-Drop](https://github.com/CASE-Lab-UMD/LLM-Drop)). A second loop visit, however, reduces the attention sink and redirects attention to content ([SMELT](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.01343v1)), so later-pass attention may be doing distinct work and must be tested pass by pass. Building KV once also accelerates prefill when designed in: YOCO exits prefill after its self-decoder and skips at least half of it ([YOCO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.05254)), Universal YOCO loops only its sliding-window layers ([YOCO-U](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01220)), and IFM reports distilled prefill up to 1.79× faster plus a learned depth prior whose 3× smaller KV cache matches fixed-depth training ([xllm-loop](https://github.com/ifm-ai/xllm-loop)) *[secondary]*. Bolted on afterwards it fails, as Ouro's post-hoc prefill sharing showed. 2:4 sparsity is the weakest arithmetic lever on these devices. An Orin user saw identical latency on a 2:4 YOLOv7 ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/stuctured-sparsity-2-4-does-not-improve-inference-performance-on-jetson-orin/263685)) *[secondary]*, a TensorRT-LLM build silently bypassed sparse kernels for 0% gain ([Model-Optimizer #1974](https://github.com/NVIDIA/Model-Optimizer/issues/1974)), and one-shot 2:4 pruning doubles a 7B model's perplexity, 10.17 vs 5.12 ([Wanda](https://github.com/locuslab/wanda)), an error a loop would re-apply every pass.

Per-frame vision has the strongest evidence that looped encoders tolerate fewer passes, because ALBERT is a fully weight-shared BERT: with patience-based exits it ran **2.0× fewer layers at a macro score of 81.6 vs 81.9**, a count rather than wall-clock ([arXiv 2105.13792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.13792)). Edge-RecViT combines a fully shared recursive layer with per-token depth and lands **within 0.3% top-1 of DeiT at 30.5% fewer FLOPs** ([Edge-RecViT](https://github.com/RicardoLee510520/Edge-RecViT)). A-ViT's per-token halting raised DeiT-T and DeiT-S throughput by 62% and 38% at a 0.3% drop ([A-ViT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07658)), though its released code does not physically drop halted tokens ([A-ViT code](https://github.com/NVlabs/A-ViT)). A frame-level exit (one, two or three passes per frame) keeps shapes static and suits NPUs, WebGPU and TensorRT, whereas per-token depth needs fixed-capacity top-k selection, which in vision has no causality problem; expect 1.2–1.5× at under 1% loss *[inference]*.

Token reduction gives large measured GPU gains that shrink or reverse on edge devices. Training-free merging doubles ViT-L@512 and ViT-H@518 throughput at a 0.2–0.3 point loss but costs about 2 points at ~1.65× on 224-pixel ViT-S and ViT-B, measured on a V100 ([ToMe](https://github.com/facebookresearch/ToMe)). Learned pruning cuts 31–37% of DeiT-S FLOPs at a 0.2–0.5 point loss after 30 fine-tuning epochs ([DynamicViT](https://github.com/raoyongming/DynamicViT)), and DINOv2 features are so self-similar that merging hurts them more ([arXiv 2609.18279](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.18279)). On TX2 and AGX Orin at batch 1, latency is a step function of token count, and other reduction methods *increased* latency by 2–30% while a latency-aware schedule cut it by 9–26% ([arXiv 2407.05941](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.05941)). Fine-tuned token and head pruning cut DeiT-B latency on AGX Orin by 19–43% ([HEART-ViT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.20120)), and one training-free merge cut latency 60% on a Raspberry Pi 5 but only 20% on an H100 ([MPM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.05718)). Looping adds an option no source has tested: merge or drop tokens after pass 1, run passes 2 and 3 on the smaller set, then unmerge before a dense head. Nothing about shared weights prevents it, and the only related work found is a title on post-training merging for recursive ViTs ([survey list](https://github.com/ArminAzizi98/looped-lm-survey)) *[inference]*. At high resolution, attention rather than token count is the cost; ReLU linear attention lets EfficientViT segment 1024×2048 Cityscapes frames in 9.9–81.8 ms on Orin ([EfficientViT](https://github.com/mit-han-lab/efficientvit/blob/master/applications/efficientvit_seg/README.md)), but these hierarchical blocks loop only within a stage, and adopting them means a new stage A.

## Video streams: reuse the last frame before shrinking this one

In a video stream, most of each frame has already been computed. Recomputing only the tokens that changed removes far more work than any per-frame lever, and the measured wall-clock gains, though smaller than the FLOP savings, are the largest in this report outside decode.

| Rank | Video lever | Expected on edge devices *[inference]* | Strongest evidence | Training |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Temporal reuse: recompute changed tokens, keep per-pass buffers, refresh densely at intervals | 1.3–2.5× | Eventful: 2–4× less compute, ViTDet 86.6 → 38.1 ms on an RTX 3090 (measured); MaskVD 3.14× fewer FLOPs but 1.5× lower latency; RLT +35% throughput without training | Mostly none; temporal heads may need fine-tuning |
| 2 | Warm start from the previous frame's state, with a frame-level exit | 1.3–2.5× on slowly changing scenes | StreamDEQ more than 3× faster on an implicit model; RecSlowFast +279% FPS on a desktop GPU | Stage A augmentation |
| 3 | Per-frame levers (passes, tokens, precision) | As in the previous section, on changed content | Previous section | As above |
| 4 | Depth-wise batching across frames or cameras | 1.2–2× throughput when one frame underfills the device; latency rises | None | None |

Eventful Transformers re-process only tokens that changed significantly since the last frame, convert existing ViTs often without retraining, expose a run-time compute budget, and save 2–4× compute with minor accuracy loss on video detection and action recognition. Measured on an RTX 3090, ViTDet fell from **86.6 to 38.1 ms** and ViViT from 950 to 545 ms, using an unfused PyTorch implementation that its authors call likely sub-optimal ([Eventful](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13494)). MaskVD skips up to 80% of regions for **3.14× fewer FLOPs but only 1.5× lower latency** ([MaskVD](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.12067)), the typical gap between counted and measured savings when gather and scatter run without custom kernels. Run-Length Tokenization removes temporally repeated patches before the model for about 35% higher throughput at a 0.1% drop without training ([RLT](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05222)), and Déjà Vu's trained reuse decisions reach 2.64× within 2% error ([Déjà Vu](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2506.14107)) *[secondary]*. A looped encoder needs reuse buffers per block-pass, not per unique block, so buffer memory scales with all six passes and the memory saving of weight sharing does not carry over *[inference]*. Errors also accumulate across both frames and passes; periodic dense frames bound them, as Déjà Vu's codec-like I/P/B scheduling does.

Warm start is the loop-native lever for video. StreamDEQ seeds each frame's fixed-point solve with the previous frame's representation and runs **more than 3× faster** at equal accuracy on video segmentation and detection ([StreamDEQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.13492)); DEQ-Flow's cross-frame reuse is reported at about 1.6× in one excerpt that a second search could not re-locate ([DEQ-Flow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08442)); and RecSlowFast reports 279% higher average FPS from reusing prior-frame hidden states with fewer recurrent steps ([RecSlowFast](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v243/wang24a/wang24a.pdf)). Those are implicit or recurrent models. A looped transformer distilled to run exactly three passes from the input embedding is not a fixed-point solver, and feeding it last frame's state changes its input distribution, so warm start must be trained in *[inference]*. Stage A would mix fresh and previous-frame initial states with noise, re-inject the current frame's input at every pass, condition FiLM on the actual pass index, and add a fixed-point or spectral regularizer so passes settle, the way Jacobian regularization cut DEQ solver iterations from 17 to 6 ([Bai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.14342)). A frame-level exit then runs a single pass on frames whose state barely moves. No source has measured warm-started looped transformers on video.

Depth-wise batching, useless for a single decoding user, becomes exact for vision. Frames do not depend on one another's outputs, so frame f at pass 3 can share a batched call with frame f+1 at pass 2 and frame f+2 at pass 1, reading each block once per step. That raises throughput only when a single frame leaves the device underused, as with small models, low resolutions or several cameras on one Jetson, and it delays each result by up to two steps *[inference]*.

## Stage A buys the run-time knobs, stage B fits the switches

The user's pipeline distills a looped student from a full teacher: stage A matches the teacher's internal outputs, with gradients from every pass summed on the shared blocks, and stage B fits a small head. Each compute lever needs nothing from that pipeline, a few extra loss terms in stage A or heads in stage B, or a new architecture and therefore a new stage A. The cheap additions are worth making together, because they create the knobs (truncation, exits, drafts, warm start) that the run-time levers turn.

Stage A is where depth flexibility is bought. A readout loss after every pass, applying the stage-B head to each pass's output and matching it to the teacher's logits with weight toward the final pass, is Ouro's per-step exit loss and LayerSkip's shared early-exit head ([LayerSkip](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16710)), and it is what makes pass 1 draftable and passes 1–2 exitable. Three further additions make truncation safe: sampling the number of passes per batch, as Huginn trained with a random recurrence count ([Huginn](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05171)) and STARS pairs random loop sampling with Jacobian spectral regularization ([STARS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26733)) *[secondary]*; conditioning FiLM on the actual normalized depth, as LARM does ([LARM](https://github.com/idiap/LARM)); and distilling the student's own full-depth output into sampled intermediate exits, as ELT does ([ELT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09168)). The KV policy (ragged, last-pass or first-pass writes) must be fixed in stage A's attention masks, because it determines what exits cost later, and for video stage A is also where warm-start augmentation goes. The teacher supplies something a from-scratch looped model lacks: per-token labels of where the student's pass-k readout already agrees with the teacher, the distillation analogue of Think-at-Hard's oracle labels from a reference model's mismatches ([TaH](https://github.com/thu-nics/TaH)) *[inference]*.

Stage B fits the switches with the backbone frozen. Per-pass heads or a small-vocabulary draft head, and an exit gate trained on lookahead labels ("does one more pass lower the KL to the teacher?"), follow Think-at-Hard's two-stage recipe of oracle-labeled iterations followed by an MLP decider on a frozen backbone, its successor's lookahead supervision ([TaH](https://github.com/thu-nics/TaH)), and DeeBERT's order of fine-tuning the backbone before the exits ([DeeBERT](https://github.com/castorini/DeeBERT)). A calibration stage after B then fits what a quantized deployment needs: Hessians accumulated across all passes, per-pass activation scales, per-block-per-pass sparsity thresholds, exit thresholds and token-merge rates, each chosen at the deployed depth.

| ReLU FFN for 80–90% sparsity | Yes | Student architecture at the start of stage A | Retrofitting took under 150B tokens ([TurboSparse](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05955v1)) |
| QAT for 4-bit activations; 2:4 masks | Yes | Late stage A | A fraction of stage A |
| MoE, cross-loop parallelism, local-attention passes, KV by design, linear attention | Yes, new architecture | New stage A | Dense-to-looped conversion took 15–60B tokens at 1B scale ([RRT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20672)) |

Re-distillation budgets for edge-sized students are unknown. The anchors are larger models: MoEfication builds experts from existing FFN weights and needs only light router training for a 2× FLOP cut ([MoEfication](https://github.com/thunlp/MoEfication)), while sparse upcycling beat dense models using about half the original pretraining cost ([sparse upcycling](https://mlanthology.org/iclr/2023/komatsuzaki2023iclr-sparse)). Any architectural change therefore costs at least a full stage A *[inference]*.

## Levers compound sub-multiplicatively, and seven pairings collide

In principle the levers multiply, because latency is roughly passes × tokens per pass × cost per token-pass, plus fixed overhead, and each family acts on a different factor. In practice they overlap. Exits and FlashLoop's token-sparse updates both skip converged tokens' late passes (FlashLoop's sparse updates are an implicit per-token exit); speculation and exits both profit from easy tokens; activation sparsity and 4-bit weights both shrink bytes but rarely share a kernel, since TEAL's kernels are FP16-only; and temporal reuse removes the same static content that token merging would. Budgets should therefore assume sub-multiplicative stacking *[inference]*. Seven pairings collide outright.

| Pairing | What goes wrong | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| Early exit × per-pass KV | Later tokens need KV from passes an exited token skipped, which is why Ouro's shipped code runs all loops | Choose ragged, recursion-wise or shared KV in stage A; never back-fill |
| Early exit × static NPU graphs | Phone NPUs and the DLA run no rolled loops; per-token exits need per-pass graphs and a CPU call for each | Frame- or sequence-level exits; per-pass subgraphs sharing weights; on NPU decode, prefer speculation |
| Per-pass scales × static NPU graphs | One compiled activation encoding per op | Unroll, which per-pass norms, FiLM or LoRA already force; deduplicate weights across graphs; dynamic scales only on GPU and CPU |
| Self-speculation × cross-loop parallelism | Both consume the same idle arithmetic of memory-bound decode | Choose one |
| Self-speculation or exits × a large output head | Each draft or logit-based check reads the head | Small draft head; hidden-state exit criteria |
| Token or pass sparsity × static shapes | Data-dependent counts force dynamic shapes or padding | Fixed-capacity top-k; for autoregressive models, a causal predictor |
| Aggressive arithmetic × loop reuse | Quantization or pruning error is re-applied on every pass | 8-bit or int16 activations, block-scaled 4-bit, all-pass calibration, pruning inside stage A |

The first two collisions decide the architecture of any exit scheme. Exits only save work if no token ever back-fills KV it skipped, and that is a training-time choice, because Ouro shows that a policy bolted on afterwards can succeed (last-loop KV at decode) or collapse (first-loop KV, 18.73 GSM8K) depending on which passes were trained to be read ([Ouro](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25741)). On phones, no source shows Hexagon, the Neural Engine or the DLA executing a rolled loop, and TensorRT's ONNX `Loop` support lists no INT8 or FP8 types ([onnx-tensorrt](https://github.com/onnx/onnx-tensorrt/blob/main/docs/operators.md)), so a quantized looped model ships unrolled. What these stacks do support is weight deduplication across graphs: QNN shares weights across up to 64 graphs in one context binary ([Qualcomm KBA](https://docs.qualcomm.com/doc/KBA-250306025001/KBA-250306025001_REV_1_QNN_Weight_Sharing_Feature.pdf)), Core ML multifunction models deduplicate weights by hash ([coremltools](https://github.com/apple/coremltools/blob/main/docs-guides/source/multifunction-models.md)), and ANEMLL's deduplication shrank a palettized two-function package from 862 to 472 MB ([ANEMLL](https://github.com/Anemll/Anemll/blob/main/docs/anemll-dedup.md)). For this user the per-pass-scales conflict is therefore mostly moot: per-pass norms, FiLM or LoRA already make each pass a distinct op, so the graph is unrolled anyway, and per-pass activation encodings come at no extra structural cost *[inference]*. The real NPU cost falls on exits, which need one subgraph per pass boundary and one host call per pass, on hardware whose decode can already be dispatch-bound.

The rest are budgeting tensions rather than collisions. MoE spends back the RAM that looping saved and, with divergent routing, touches more distinct experts per token, which defeats residency; it suits Thor and Orin AGX better than phones and browsers *[inference]*. Temporal reuse needs buffers for every block-pass, so its memory grows with pass count. Warm start conflicts with a student trained only from fresh embeddings until stage A mixes initial states. Depth-wise batching across frames trades latency for throughput. Residency under A→B→A→B order needs both blocks cached together, and the back-to-back order that makes residency easy changes the network's function.

### Recommended stack per target

Stacked, these should give roughly 1.5–3× on decode (speculation or cross-loop parallelism, times fusion, times KV policy and exits); 2–6× on prefill on Thor and on phone CPUs and NPUs, but about 1.3–1.6× on Orin and Apple WebGPU; 1.5–3× on per-frame vision on the Neural Engine and Hexagon, and about 1.3–2× on Orin; and another 1.3–2.5× for video on slowly changing scenes *[inference]*.

Measurement discipline matters as much as the levers. Adaptive compute must be benchmarked at batch 1, because batched caches leak deeper KV and flatter exits ([Huginn code](https://github.com/seal-rg/recurrent-pretraining/blob/main/recpre/raven_modeling_minimal.py)). Every lever should report counted and measured savings side by side, since MaskVD's 3.14× FLOPs became 1.5× latency and token pruning on Orin can turn a FLOP saving into a slowdown. Jetson runs need locked clocks, speculation needs a check of token-identical greedy output, and short and long contexts must be reported separately because KV policy only pays at length.

## Conclusion

Looping turns parameters into depth, and depth into a bill paid in arithmetic, bytes and dispatches; what changes between regimes is which currency the bill is paid in. Where bytes bind, shared weights are an under-used asset, because work from several depths can ride on one read. That is why speculation and cross-loop parallelism are the only loop-specific levers with large measured gains, and why depth-wise batching, the lever loop papers first advertised, does nothing for a single user. Where arithmetic binds, looping is a pure liability, and its one exploitable property is that later passes are partly redundant, which exits, FlashLoop's sparse late passes and temporal reuse harvest. For edge students the most decision-relevant finding is a negative one: in small models with large vocabularies, the output head and the KV cache can outweigh the loop and cap every loop lever, so the first number to measure is a byte budget, not a speedup.

For the user, the cheapest compute reduction is bought before deployment, inside the distillation run that already exists. A few extra loss terms and heads (per-pass readouts, random pass counts, depth-conditioned FiLM, a fixed KV policy, warm-start augmentation) cost about one stage-A budget and turn a rigid three-pass student into one that can be truncated, exited, drafted from and warm-started. Architectural bets such as cross-loop parallelism, KV by design, MoE and linear attention should wait until the training-free stack has been measured on the device. The field's largest gap is also the user's opportunity: nobody has published a loop-specific speedup on a phone, a browser or a Jetson, so the first clean on-device numbers would be new evidence rather than a deployment check.
