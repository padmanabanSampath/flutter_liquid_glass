## 1. The cube group and its Cayley graph: order, generators, metrics, God's number, distance distribution, element orders, commutators and conjugates

### Takeaway
The cube group has 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 ≈ 4.3×10^19 elements. It is generated by 6 face quarter-turns: 18 generators in the half-turn metric (HTM), 12 in the quarter-turn metric (QTM). Its Cayley graph has diameter 20 in HTM (proved 2010) and 26 in QTM (proved 2014). Almost all positions lie at distance 18–19, so a "typical" scramble is close to the worst case. The ball grows about 13.35× per extra move, and the diameter is only about 2.5 moves above the bare counting bound (≈17.4). Element orders range from 1 to 1260: R has order 4, RU has order 105, and the maximum is 1260. Commutators and conjugates are how you build small, targeted operations (for example a 3-cycle touching 3 of the 20 moving cubies) out of big, messy generators.

### Cited Findings
**Group order and structure**
- Order = 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 = 2^27·3^14·5^3·7^2·11. [S] [CTF writeup](https://ctftime.org/writeup/22581). The factorization was re-multiplied here [C]. Equivalent closed form: 8!·3^7·12!·2^11 / 2 = 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 [C].
- Schreier–Sims on the 6 face generators gives exactly 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 [C]. The 2-generator subgroup <R, U> has order 73,483,200 [C].
- The cube group is non-abelian (FR ≠ RF). [S] [search summary over Wikipedia "Rubik's Cube group"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubik%27s_Cube_group)

**Metrics and God's number**
- HTM/FTM: any face turn of 90°, 180° or 270° counts as 1 move, giving 18 generators. QTM: only 90° turns count, so a half-turn costs 2, giving 12 generators. "If a half-twist is counted as two quarter-twists, then God's number is 26." [S] [cube20.org snapshot (Brandeis PDF copy)](https://www.cs.brandeis.edu/~storer/JimPuzzles/RUBIK/Rubik3x3x3/READING/GodsNumberIs20.pdf)
- God's number is 20 in HTM. The proof was completed in July 2010 by Tomas Rokicki, Herbert Kociemba, Morley Davidson and John Dethridge. [S] [cube20.org](https://www.cube20.org/); [S] [Brandeis copy](https://www.cs.brandeis.edu/~storer/JimPuzzles/RUBIK/Rubik3x3x3/READING/GodsNumberIs20.pdf)
- Proof method: the positions were split into 2,217,093,120 sets of 19,508,428,800 positions each. Symmetry and set covering cut the number of sets to solve to 55,882,296, which took about 35 CPU-years (on Google machines). Within each set they found solutions of length ≤20, not optimal solutions. [S] [Brandeis copy of cube20.org](https://www.cs.brandeis.edu/~storer/JimPuzzles/RUBIK/Rubik3x3x3/READING/GodsNumberIs20.pdf); [S] [cube20.org](https://www.cube20.org/)
- These sets are exactly the cosets of Kociemba's subgroup G1 = <U, D, R2, L2, F2, B2>. Schreier–Sims gives |G1| = 19,508,428,800 and index [G : G1] = 2,217,093,120 [C], matching the cube20 numbers.
- God's number is 26 in QTM, shown in 2014 by Tomas Rokicki and Morley Davidson with about 29 CPU-years of idle time at the Ohio Supercomputer Center. Superflip composed with fourspot was "the first position proven to require 26 moves". [S] [cube20.org/qtm](https://cube20.org/qtm/). Only that position (and its two rotations) needs 26 quarter-turns. [S] [Cleve Moler, MathWorks blog, 2022](https://blogs.mathworks.com/cleve/2022/09/05/rubiks-cube-superflips-and-gods-number/)
- Superflip was the first position proved to need 20 HTM moves. An optimal 20f* solve of superflip took Kociemba's Python optimal solver ≈7.3 h and 75,047,390,029 generated nodes. [P] [hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)
- History of the bounds: a 2008 paper proved 25 moves suffice and noted that "more than 36,000 distinct positions" were then known to need ≥20 [S] [Rokicki, "Twenty-Five Moves Suffice", arXiv 0803.3435](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0803.3435). In 1992 the bounds were 18 (lower) and 37 (upper) [S] [cube20.org history table](https://www.cube20.org/).

**Distance distribution (sphere sizes of the Cayley graph)**
- HTM positions at distance 0–4: 1, 18, 243, 3,240, 43,239. [S] [Jerry Bryan's tables](https://www.jerrybryan.com/math/rubikresults.html); [S] [OEIS A080601](https://oeis.org/A080601). OEIS's comment says "half-turn" while the numbers are face-turn counts. These are the same metric (FTM = HTM), so this is a naming difference, not an error.
- The number of canonical move sequences (no two consecutive turns of the same face; commuting opposite-face turns in a fixed order) for n = 1..8 is 18, 243, 3,240, 43,254, 577,368, 7,706,988, 102,876,480, 1,373,243,544. The ratio per extra move tends to 13.348 [C]. The 43,254 sequences of length 4 against 43,239 positions at distance 4 [S] means the first non-trivial coincidences appear at depth 4.
- Kociemba independently: each extra move multiplies the number of maneuvers by "a factor of about 13.3". [P] [RubiksCube-OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)
- Counting lower bound on the diameter: log_13.348(4.3252×10^19) ≈ 17.45 [C], against a true diameter of 20.
- Mass concentration: "nearly all 4.3 × 10^19 states land at d = 18 or 19, while only 4.9 × 10^8 states sit at d = 20". [Sec, weak: a blog post of ChatGPT Q&A](https://generic.wordpress.soton.ac.uk/skywritings/2024/12/30/8-questions-to-chatgpt-about-rubiks-cubes/). This is consistent with Kociemba's primary statement that the chance a random cube needs 20 moves is "in the order of 1/10^11" [P] [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver), since 4.9×10^8 / 4.3×10^19 ≈ 1.1×10^-11 [C].
- Known distance-20 HTM positions: "all 102,764,490 known distance-20 positions as of 5 April 2024". An older cube20 snapshot said about twelve million. [S] [cube20.org/distance20s](https://cube20.org/distance20s/)
- QTM table on cube20.org/qtm: 5,442,351,625,028 positions at QTM distance 13, and the distance-26 entry is marked with "?". [S] [cube20.org/qtm](https://cube20.org/qtm/); [S] [Jerry Bryan](https://www.jerrybryan.com/math/rubikresults.html)
- Typical random-cube optimal length: Korf (1997) found a median optimal length of 18 moves [S] [Korf 1997, AAAI](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/1997/korf1997aaai-finding). Ten random cubes solved optimally by Kociemba's Python solver averaged 17.80 HTM, all 17 or 18 [P] [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver). DeepCubeA's 1,000 QTM test cubes have an optimal mean of 20.64 QTM [S] [Takano, arXiv 2106.03157 results table](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.03157).
- 2024 and 2026 work on diameters/growth: "Probabilistic estimates of the diameters of the Rubik's Cube groups" (arXiv 2404.07337, 2024) and "God numbers for Graphs, Games and Groups" (arXiv 2605.20243, May 2026) both exist [S] ([2404.07337](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.07337v5), [2605.20243](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.20243)). Neither could be read.

**Element orders**
- Computed in the facelet model [C]: ord(R) = 4, ord(R2) = 2, ord(RU) = 105, ord(RU′) = 63, ord(RU2) = 30, ord(R2U2) = 6, ord(R U R′ U′) = 6, ord(R U2 D′ B D′) = 1260.
- The maximum element order is 1260, with example R U2 D′ B D′. [S] [IBM community post](https://community.ibm.com/community/user/blogs/moloy-de1/2021/03/10/points-to-ponder); [S] [Wikipedia, via search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubik%27s_Cube_group)
- Alan Bawden's 1980 Cube-Lovers proof that no element exceeds 1260 works by case elimination. It assumes fixed centres; if centre orientation counts, the bound becomes 2520. [S] [Cube-Lovers, Bawden 1980](https://www.math.rwth-aachen.de/~Martin.Schoenert/Cube-Lovers/Alan_Bawden__1260.html); [S] [1260 vs 2520](https://www.math.rwth-aachen.de/~Martin.Schoenert/Cube-Lovers/Alan_Bawden__1260_vs._2520.html)
- RU has order 105 (also computed here, [C]). The same page claims 73 distinct element orders occur; I did not verify that. [S] [cuberoot.me order-of-element](https://www.cuberoot.me/math/group/order-of-element)

**Commutators and conjugates** (standard definitions: commutator [X,Y] = X Y X⁻¹ Y⁻¹; conjugate Y X Y⁻¹. Examples computed here, [C])
- A face turn R moves 8 cubies (20 stickers). RU moves 13 cubies. The commutator [R,U] = R U R′ U′ moves 7 cubies and has order 6. [R′,F] = R′ F R F′ also moves 7 cubies with order 6.
- The commutator of the 3-move sequence (R U R′) with D, i.e. R U R′ D R U′ R′ D′, moves exactly 3 cubies (9 stickers) and has order 3: a pure corner 3-cycle. Two big generators combine into an operation that touches 3 of the 20 moving cubies.
- Conjugation preserves order and cycle type. U·[R,U]·U′ has order 6 and moves 7 cubies, the same as [R,U] [C]. Conjugating relocates where an operation acts without changing what it does.
- (R2 U2)^3 moves 4 cubies (two edge swaps) and has order 2 [C].

### Inferences
- **Reachability is cheap in moves but expensive in coverage.** 18 generators and ≤20 compositions reach 4.3×10^19 states, and the diameter is only ~2.5 above the counting bound. The cube's Cayley graph is close to as "efficient" as an 18-regular graph can be. For a looped model: a few blocks composed T times can in principle span an enormous function family. But the cube also shows that almost all of that family sits near maximal depth, so most targets need nearly the full depth budget. Short paths are rare (depth-10 positions are ~10^-8 of the group). This hints that the number of loops should be set by the hard tail of inputs, or made adaptive, rather than by the median input. Adaptive depth itself is covered elsewhere.
- **Order and periodicity.** Repeating a single fixed move sequence returns to the start after its order (4 for R, 105 for RU, at most 1260). Iterating the same block cycle (A→B→A→B…) on a finite state space is a single group element repeated: it cycles and never explores. In the cube, exploration comes from varying which generator is applied at each step, never from repeating one word. This is an analogy, not a proven property of continuous networks.
- **Commutators as "targeted edits".** [X,Y] cancels everywhere except where X and Y interact, which yields small-support operations (3-cycles) from large-support generators. A looped-network counterpart would be block pairs whose composite does a small, localized residual edit. I found no ML literature testing this directly.
- **Metric choice changes the depth budget.** The same group has diameter 20 or 26 depending on whether half-turns are one generator or two. Adding "macro" generators (squares of moves) cuts depth by ~23%. A loose analogy: giving the looped model a fused or "double-step" block option. That is an inference only.

## 2. Thistlethwaite's algorithm, Kociemba's two-phase algorithm, Korf's optimal solver, symmetry reduction

### Takeaway
All three classic solvers turn one search over 4.3×10^19 states into searches over small quotient spaces. **Thistlethwaite (1981):** 4 phases down a nested subgroup chain, with fewer allowed moves each phase. The originally proved bound was 52 moves, and exact phase maxima later gave 45. **Kociemba (1992):** 2 phases through the single subgroup G1 = <U, D, R2, L2, F2, B2>. Phase 1 solves a 2.2×10^9-state coset coordinate and phase 2 a 1.95×10^10-state subgroup, each with IDA* guided by exact BFS distance tables. It keeps searching for shorter total solutions, giving an anytime near-optimal solver (≈18.4–19.3 HTM average in 0.1–10 s in Python). **Korf (1997):** first optimal solutions of random cubes, via IDA* with admissible pattern-database heuristics (max over corner and edge subproblems). It took ~2 days per cube in 1997. Reid's symmetry-reduced phase-1-table heuristic later gave much faster optimal solving.

### Cited Findings
**Thistlethwaite**
- Coset sizes (stage indices) per Jaap Scherphuis: 2,048 (G0→G1, edge orientation, 2^11), 1,082,565 (G1→G2, C(12,4)·3^7), 29,400 (G2→G3, C(8,4)²·2·3), 663,552 (G3→G4, 4!^5/12). [S] [Jaap's Puzzle Page, Thistlethwaite](https://www.jaapsch.net/puzzles/thistle.htm). Dan Hoey's 1992 Cube-Lovers post gives the last two indices in the opposite order. Jaap's factorizations, and the subgroup computations below, support Jaap's order. [S] [Hoey 1992](https://www.math.rwth-aachen.de/~Martin.Schoenert/Cube-Lovers/Dan_Hoey__Re__Rubik's_Cube,_the_minimax_number_of_moves.html)
- Computed subgroup orders [C], using the standard Thistlethwaite generator sets:
  - G0 = <U, D, L, R, F, B>: 43,252,003,274,489,856,000
  - G1 = <L, R, F, B, U2, D2>: 21,119,142,223,872,000 (index 2,048)
  - G2 = <L, R, F2, B2, U2, D2>: 19,508,428,800 (index 1,082,565)
  - G3 = <L2, R2, F2, B2, U2, D2>: 663,552 (index 29,400)
  - G4 = {e}: index 663,552

  This confirms Jaap's ordering. The product of the four indices equals the group order.
- Move bounds: Thistlethwaite originally bounded the phases at 7 / 13 / 15 / 17 HTM, total 52. Hoey's table also has a "Best 1980" column of 7 / 10 / 13 / 15 (total 45), the exact phase maxima found later by computer. [S] [Hoey 1992](https://www.math.rwth-aachen.de/~Martin.Schoenert/Cube-Lovers/Dan_Hoey__Re__Rubik's_Cube,_the_minimax_number_of_moves.html); [S] [cuberoot.me Thistlethwaite](https://cuberoot.me/math/group/thistlethwaite)
- Thistlethwaite's G2 (order 19,508,428,800) has exactly the same order as Kociemba's G1 [C]. Kociemba merged Thistlethwaite's first two phases into one and his last two into one.

**Kociemba two-phase**
- Phase 1 maps a cube into G1 using three coordinates: corner orientation "twist" (2,187 = 3^7), edge orientation "flip" (2,048 = 2^11) and the UD-slice edge set (495 = C(12,4)). The cube is in G1 when all three are zero. Phase-1 space = 2,187 × 2,048 × 495 ≈ 2.2×10^9 states. [S] [kociemba.org two-phase coordinates](https://kociemba.org/math/twophase.htm); [S] [cuberoot.me Kociemba](https://cuberoot.me/code/algorithms/kociemba)
- 2,187 × 2,048 × 495 = 2,217,093,120 = [G : G1] [C]. The phase-1 state space is exactly the coset space G/G1 used by the God's-number proof.
- Phase 2 has only 10 legal moves: U U′ U2 D D′ D2 L2 R2 F2 B2. Its coordinates are corner permutation (8! = 40,320), the 8 U/D-layer edges' permutation (8! = 40,320) and the UD-slice permutation (4! = 24, from a "sorted" slice coordinate with range 0–11,879 that takes only 0–23 in phase 2). Phase-2 states = 40,320·40,320·24/2 ≈ 1.95×10^10. [S] [kociemba.org two-phase coordinates](https://kociemba.org/math/twophase.htm). The /2 is the parity constraint, and 19,508,428,800 = |G1| [C].
- Solution quality versus time, from Kociemba's Python implementation (AMD Ryzen 7 3700X; 1,000 random cubes per row) [P] [RubiksCube-TwophaseSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-TwophaseSolver):

  | Interpreter | Time per cube | Average length (HTM) |
  |---|---|---|
  | CPython | 30 s | 18.63 |
  | CPython | 10 s | 18.79 |
  | CPython | 1 s | 19.27 |
  | CPython | 0.1 s | 20.02 |
  | PyPy | 10 s | 18.37 |
  | PyPy | 1 s | 18.76 |
  | PyPy | 0.1 s | 19.33 |
  | PyPy | 0.01 s | 20.45 |

  "To achieve an average solution length below 19 moves, approximately 10 seconds per cube are sufficient with CPython, whereas only about 1 second is required with PyPy." It solves random cubes in <20 moves on average "within a few seconds, even on … Raspberry Pi 3".
- The two-phase solver is anytime. `solve(cubestring, max_length, timeout)` returns the shortest solution found when the timeout hits. `solveto` solves to any goal pattern, not just the solved state. [P] [TwophaseSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-TwophaseSolver)

**Korf (1997) and later optimal solvers**
- Korf's AAAI 1997 paper reported the first optimal solutions to random cubes. It used IDA* with a lower-bound heuristic from "pattern databases": tables storing exact move counts to solve subgoals (subsets of cubies). The median optimal length was 18. [S] [Korf 1997](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/1997/korf1997aaai-finding); [S] [Korf PDF (Princeton mirror)](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall06/cos402/papers/korfrubik.pdf)
- Korf used three databases: 8 corners, 6 of the 12 edges, and the other 6 edges. The heuristic is the max of the three, which keeps it admissible. [S] [search summary of Korf 1997 / secondary](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall06/cos402/papers/korfrubik.pdf). Database state counts: corners 8!·3^7 = 88,179,840; a 6-edge subset 12!/6!·2^6 = 42,577,920 [C]. These are the commonly cited Korf PDB sizes, but I did not see them stated in the paper text.
- Kociemba's account of Korf's run: Sun Ultra-SPARC Model 1, pruning tables of about 80 MB, about 700,000 nodes/s, ~1.2×10^11 nodes for a full depth-17 search, about 2 days of computation, 10 random cubes. [P] [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)
- Michael Reid (1997) used the phase-1 pruning table of the two-phase algorithm as the heuristic. The phase-1 target group has the 16-fold D4h symmetry, so the table compresses by about 16×, and it can be applied in three directions (three axis conjugates), which strengthens the heuristic. [P] [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver), citing [Reid's optimal solver page](https://www.cflmath.com/Rubik/optimal_solver.html)
- Kociemba's Python optimal solver uses a 794 MB pruning table, 24× larger than Reid's 34 MB. Its target subgroup also fixes the 4 UD-slice edges in position, and applying it in 3 directions leaves only corner permutation unconstrained. Tables total ~955 MB of disk and take 13 min (PyPy) or 8 h (CPython) to build. It generates ~2.5×10^6 nodes/s. Ten random cubes took 37–1,167 s each (3,841 s total), mean optimal length 17.80. The hardest known cube20.org position took about 3 h (2.94×10^10 nodes). [P] [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)
- Iterative-deepening profile of one optimal solve [P] [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver):

  | Depth | Nodes | Time |
  |---|---|---|
  | 15 | 1.29×10^6 | 0.75 s |
  | 16 | 1.78×10^7 | 7.06 s |
  | 17 | 2.45×10^8 | 98 s |
  | 18 | 5.75×10^8 | 231 s |

  Each deeper iteration costs ~13× more until the solution depth is reached.

**Symmetry reduction**
- The cube has 48 spatial symmetries (I could not fetch a source that states this). Tables are stored per symmetry class.
- min2phase (Chen Shuang's two-phase implementation) uses a twist×flip pruning table of 324 twist-symmetry classes × 2,048 flips = 663,552 cells at 4 bits each, 332 KB. The 324 is a symmetry-class count, not 2,187/16. [S] [cuberoot.me min2phase](https://cuberoot.me/code/algorithms/min2phase)
- Symmetry is also how the God's-number proof shrank 2,217,093,120 cosets to 55,882,296 (together with set cover). [S] [Brandeis copy of cube20.org](https://www.cs.brandeis.edu/~storer/JimPuzzles/RUBIK/Rubik3x3x3/READING/GodsNumberIs20.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Phase decomposition = solving in a tower of quotients with shrinking generator sets.** Each phase only has to reach a subgroup (an invariant set) and may then use only moves that preserve it. Phase 2's 10 moves never leave G1. Total length is at most the sum of the phase bounds. That sum is not optimal (52 or 45 versus 20; two-phase 18–20 versus 17.8 optimal), and the gap closes when phase 1 is allowed to continue past its first solution (Kociemba's anytime loop).
- Mapping to staged distillation of looped models:
  - Teacher internals act as "intermediate subgroups": hidden states the student must reach after k loops.
  - Phase-specific blocks correspond to phase-specific move sets.
  - Requiring later loops to preserve what earlier loops fixed is the analogue of G1-preserving moves.
  - The cube's lesson: greedy phase-optimal is not globally optimal. Joint search over where to cross the intermediate target (Kociemba's continued phase-1 enumeration) recovers most of the gap.
- **Admissible heuristics as max over independent subproblem distances (Korf).** The cheap, exact signal is "distance to solve this subset of the state", combined by max. A neural counterpart is a per-factor distance-to-go head (for example per token group or per image region) aggregated by max as a conservative halting signal. This is an inference; halting design is covered by another researcher.
- **Symmetry quotienting is the cube's biggest single compression** (~16× for D4h tables, 48 symmetries overall). For per-frame vision models, the analogue is weight-sharing or caching across known input symmetries (flips, rotations). This is speculative.

## 3. Move tables and pruning tables: what they are, sizes, how built by BFS, why they make search tractable

### Takeaway
A **move table** is a precomputed transition function on a small coordinate: `next = table[coord][move]`. It replaces permuting 20 cubies with one array lookup. A **pruning table** stores, for every value of a coordinate (or coordinate pair), the exact distance to the target subgroup. It is built by breadth-first search outward from the target using the move tables, and is an admissible lower bound for IDA*. Because ~13.35^d sequences exist at depth d, a bound that cuts even a few moves from the remaining depth prunes most of the tree. The tables are tens of KB to ~1 GB (Kociemba ~80 MB; optimal solver 794 MB; DeepCubeA's comparison optimal solver 182 GB), which is the memory-for-compute trade that neural heuristics later replaced.

### Cited Findings
- Kociemba's own wording: pruning tables "give you some heuristics value for each position how many moves there are *at least* still necessary to solve that position. If you are searching for 17 move solutions … and you generated the first 10 moves … you may stop if the pruning table tells you that you need at least 8 more moves." Optimal-solve time depends on (1) table quality, which grows with table size, and (2) nodes generated per second. [P] [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)
- Two-phase tables: ~80 MB, generated on first run, "half an hour or more". "It is precisely these computationally expensive tables that enable the algorithm to operate efficiently, usually finding near-optimal solutions." [P] [TwophaseSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-TwophaseSolver)
- Optimal-solver tables: ~955 MB total, with a 794 MB main pruning table 24× larger than Reid's 34 MB. The bigger table gives ~7× speedup in Python, and PyPy a further ~13×, about 100× combined. [P] [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver)
- Coordinate sizes that set table dimensions:
  - Phase-1 coordinates: twist 2,187, flip 2,048, UD-slice 495. Phase-1 space 2.2×10^9. [S] [kociemba.org](https://kociemba.org/math/twophase.htm)
  - Phase-2 coordinates: 40,320 / 40,320 / 24. Phase-2 space 1.95×10^10. [S] [kociemba.org](https://kociemba.org/math/twophase.htm)
  - Symmetry-reduced twist×flip table: 663,552 cells × 4 bits = 332 KB. [S] [cuberoot.me min2phase](https://cuberoot.me/code/algorithms/min2phase)
- Korf's pattern databases (corners; two disjoint 6-edge sets) store exact subgoal distances and are combined by max to stay admissible. [S] [Korf 1997](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/1997/korf1997aaai-finding). Corner and 6-edge state counts: 88,179,840 and 42,577,920 [C].
- The optimal solver that DeepCubeA and Takano compare against needs 182 GB of memory. On DeepCubeA's 1,000 test cubes it averaged 20.64 QTM moves with 2.05×10^6 nodes and 2.20 s. [S] [Takano 2023 results table, arXiv 2106.03157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.03157); [S] [same table via search](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=bnBeNFB27b)
- Pruning tables are built by BFS from the target over coordinate space. [S] [cuberoot.me Kociemba](https://cuberoot.me/code/algorithms/kociemba). Kociemba's statement that table generation is the expensive one-time step is primary. [P] [TwophaseSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-TwophaseSolver)
- Heuristic quality, in nodes: with the 794 MB table, a full depth-17 iteration generates ~2.5×10^8 nodes. Korf's ~80 MB tables needed ~1.2×10^11 nodes for depth 17 (1997). [P] [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver). That is roughly 500× fewer nodes from a better, larger, symmetry-reduced heuristic [C, ratio of the two quoted figures; different hardware and implementations].

### Inferences
- **Move tables are a "factorized transition model".** The full state is a product of small coordinates, and each move acts on each coordinate independently, so per-coordinate lookups compose. A looped-network analogue is a block whose update factorizes across small, nearly independent subspaces of the residual stream. Such updates are cheap to apply repeatedly and easy to analyze.
- **Pruning tables are exact distance-to-go on a quotient, computed once and reused for every query.** This is the distillation analogy: a teacher (BFS) produces exact targets once, and a student (the table, or a neural net) serves them at inference. The key property that makes them useful for exact search is admissibility (never overestimating). A learned distance-to-go that over-estimates causes early halting or pruning errors. Under-estimating only costs compute.
- **Heuristic quality trades almost one-for-one against search effort.** A bigger, more accurate table saves orders of magnitude of nodes (~500× above). For looped models this suggests investing parameters in a good distance or halting estimator, which may pay more than investing them in the block itself, when the loop count is adaptive.

### Gaps
- The exact sizes and entry formats (4-bit versus 2-bit mod-3 encodings) of each Kociemba table were not retrieved; kociemba.org was unreachable.
- I did not find a source quantifying how many IDA* nodes the two-phase algorithm generates per solve.

## 4. Neural / learned solvers: DeepCubeA, EfficientCube/AlphaCube, CayleyPy, Q* search, transformers and 2024–2026 work

### Takeaway
Every strong learned cube solver is **a learned distance-to-go (or move-policy) network plus search**, not a one-shot policy:
- **DeepCubeA (2019):** approximate value iteration to learn cost-to-go, then batched weighted A*. Solves 100% of test cubes; 60.3% optimal; 21.50 QTM average against 20.64 optimal.
- **EfficientCube (Takano, TMLR 2023):** trains only to predict the last scramble move on random walks from the goal, then beam search. 69.6% optimal, 21.26 average, about 10× less training compute and 2× less inference than DeepCubeA.
- **CayleyPy (Chervov et al., NeurIPS 2025 spotlight):** regresses random-walk length ("diffusion distance") and uses huge GPU beam search. >98% optimal on the 3×3×3 QTM benchmark, ~25.6× faster solving, up to 8.5× less training than the best prior learned solver. The same pipeline works on Cayley graphs up to 10^145 states.
- **Q* search (2021–2024):** puts the cost of all children into one network forward pass.

Transformer cube solvers exist (CubeTR, 2021) but are not state of the art. I found no looped or recurrent-depth cube solver. 2025–2026 cube work with LLMs is benchmarks (CubeBench, Cube Bench), which find large gaps in long-horizon state tracking.

### Cited Findings
**DeepCubeA** (Agostinelli, McAleer, Shmakov, Baldi; *Nature Machine Intelligence* 1(8):356–363, Aug 2019; online 15 Jul 2019; DOI 10.1038/s42256-019-0070-z)
- Method: approximate value iteration trains a DNN to approximate cost-to-go. Training states are generated "by starting from the goal state and randomly taking moves in reverse". The learned cost-to-go is the heuristic for weighted A*. [S] [search excerpt of paper abstract](https://cse.sc.edu/~foresta/assets/files/SolvingTheRubiksCubeWithDeepReinforcementLearningAndSearch_Final.pdf); [P] [bibliographic data in DeepCubeA repo](https://github.com/forestagostinelli/DeepCubeA)
- Results: "DeepCubeA solves 100% of all test configurations, finding a shortest path to the goal state 60.3% of the time". It also solves the 15/24/35/48-puzzles, Lights Out and Sokoban, "finding a shortest path in the majority of verifiable cases". [S] [paper PDF via search](https://cse.sc.edu/~foresta/assets/files/SolvingTheRubiksCubeWithDeepReinforcementLearningAndSearch_Final.pdf); [Sec] [UCI news, 15 Jul 2019](https://news.uci.edu/2019/07/15/uci-researchers-deep-learning-algorithm-solves-rubiks-cube-faster-than-any-human)

**EfficientCube** (Kyo Takano, "Self-Supervision is All You Need for Solving Rubik's Cube", arXiv 2106.03157 (2021); TMLR 2023)
- Method: train a DNN "on random scrambles branching from the goal state". It predicts the last scramble move from the scrambled state, and solving applies predicted inverse moves under beam search. [P] [efficientcube README abstract](https://github.com/kyo-takano/efficientcube); [S] [arXiv listing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.03157v4)
- Results (DeepCubeA test set): 21.26 average, 69.6% optimal (696/1,000), 4.18×10^6 nodes, 38.73 s normalized (483.61 s wall-clock). "10 times more efficient in training and 2.0 times in inference" than DeepCubeA (v4). Also tested on 15 Puzzle and 7×7 Lights Out, with a scaling-law study of model size and data. [S] [Takano results via search](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.03157); [P] [README](https://github.com/kyo-takano/efficientcube)
- **AlphaCube** packages Takano's compute-optimal HTM models (from the scaling-law section). Examples from the README for one scramble [P] [kyo-takano/alphacube README](https://github.com/kyo-takano/alphacube):

  | Model and device | Beam width | Solution length | Nodes | Time |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Small, CPU | 1,024 | 21 | 19,744 | 1.4 s |
  | Small, CPU | 65,536 | 19 | 968,984 | 45.7 s |
  | Large, GPU | 65,536 | 17 | 903,448 | 20.5 s |

  Solution lengths were counted here [C]. "beam_width values between 1024 and 65536 typically offer a good trade-off". This is a single anecdotal scramble, not a benchmark.

**CayleyPy / "A machine learning approach that beats Rubik's cubes"** (Chervov, Khoruzhii, Bukhal, Naghiyev, Zamkovoy, Koltsov, Cheldieva, Sychev, Lenin, Obozov, Urvanov, Romanov; arXiv 2502.13266, Feb 2025; NeurIPS 2025 spotlight)
- Method: a neural network predicts diffusion distance (the length of the random walk that produced a state) from random walks only, "zero human knowledge", and guides batched GPU beam search. [P] [khoruzhii/cayleypy-cube README](https://github.com/khoruzhii/cayleypy-cube)
- Abstract-level claims: >98% optimality on 3×3×3, against 60.3% (DeepCubeA) and 69.6% (EfficientCube). ~25.6× faster solving and up to 8.5× less training time than the most efficient prior competitor. A single agent trained on relatively few examples solves puzzles on Cayley graphs up to 10^145 states. [S] [OpenReview PDF via search](https://openreview.net/pdf/d8ed8f8817a34278fae4393e7fed6e1e25d99536.pdf); [S] [NeurIPS poster page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/120075)
- README results [P] [cayleypy-cube README](https://github.com/khoruzhii/cayleypy-cube):

  | Puzzle | Graph size | Training | Solving | Avg solution length | Solved |

**Q* search / DeepCubeAQ** (Agostinelli et al.; arXiv 2102.04518, first version 2021; ICAPS 2024 workshop version "Q* search: Heuristic search with deep Q-networks")
- A deep Q-network outputs transition cost plus heuristic for all children of a node in one forward pass, so only one node is generated per iteration. On the Rubik's cube with 1,872 meta-actions, a "157-fold increase in the size of the action space incurs less than a 4-fold increase in computation time and less than a 3-fold increase in number of nodes generated". Up to 129× faster and 1,288× fewer nodes than A*. Q* is guaranteed optimal if the heuristic never overestimates path cost and never underestimates transition cost. [S] [arXiv 2102.04518](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.04518v2); [P] [citation in deepxube README](https://github.com/forestagostinelli/deepxube)

### Inferences
- **The learned part is always a scalar or move distribution over states (a heuristic), and quality comes from search width.**
  - In AlphaCube, 64× more beam (1,024 → 65,536) bought 2 moves; a larger model bought 2 more moves at similar beam.
  - In CayleyPy, a tiny MLP with beam 2^20 reaches ~98% optimal.
  - So the network is cheap and the search budget dominates.
  - For looped models, "more loops" plays the role of "more search". The cube literature suggests the payoff curve is smooth and logarithmic in compute (two-phase: 0.01 s → 20.45, 0.1 s → 19.33, 1 s → 18.76, 10 s → 18.37 moves).
- **The three training targets form a ladder of supervision cost:**
  - DeepCubeA: Bellman/value-iteration targets (needs a target network and one-step lookahead; expensive).
  - Takano: the last scramble move (pure self-supervised classification).
  - CayleyPy: random-walk length (pure regression).

  Each simpler target was more compute-efficient. Random-walk length overestimates true distance (walks backtrack), and still worked best with wide beam search. This is a hint that monotone, consistent ranking matters more than calibrated distance.
- **Bounding training-walk length by the diameter.** CayleyPy uses K_max = 26 for the QTM cube. A looped-model counterpart is training distance-to-go only up to the maximum loop count.
- **No looped or weight-tied cube solvers found.** All reviewed solvers use a feed-forward heuristic network applied once per node; the iteration lives in the search loop, not in the network.

- DeepCubeA's network architecture, parameter count and training budget (GPU-days, number of training states) were not retrieved; the paper PDF host was unreachable.
- Takano's model sizes and the exact compute (FLOPs) behind the "10× / 2×" claims were not retrieved.
- CayleyPy's per-scramble optimality on the DeepCubeA set, its exact compute at 98% optimality, and the 25.6× / 8.5× baselines need the full paper (OpenReview/arXiv blocked).
- No 2024–2026 transformer, looped-transformer or recurrent-depth Rubik's solver with reported optimality was found. The most recent learned solvers found are MLP heuristics plus search (CayleyPy) and DeepXube.
- The McAleer et al. 2018 (DeepCube, MCTS) numbers were not retrieved.

## 5. Generalization: which mechanisms map onto neural networks (generator composition, learned distance, phase decomposition, search versus one-shot policy), and literature explicitly linking group-theoretic solving to network depth or iteration

### Takeaway
Four cube mechanisms carry over cleanly:
- **(a)** A few generators composed to depth ~20 span 4×10^19 states, and the hard tail sits near maximal depth.
- **(b)** Exact or learned distance-to-goal is the central object of every solver: Korf/Kociemba tables (exact, admissible) and DeepCubeA/Takano/CayleyPy networks (learned, not admissible).
- **(c)** Phase decomposition through a subgroup with a restricted, invariant-preserving move set turns one intractable search into two tractable ones, at a modest optimality cost that further search recovers.
- **(d)** Every strong solver pairs a cheap evaluator with search; one-shot greedy policies are never state of the art.

The closest explicit link between groups and network depth is Liu et al. (ICLR 2023). Transformers can simulate any automaton in O(log T) depth, and in O(1) depth only when its groups are solvable. The Rubik's cube group is non-solvable, so tracking a cube through T moves is exactly the kind of computation that needs depth growing with T, which is what looping supplies. I found no paper that explicitly maps Thistlethwaite/Kociemba-style phase solving onto looped-transformer depth.

### Cited Findings
- **(a) Small generator set → huge reachable set.**
  - 18 HTM generators reach all 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 states within 20 moves. [S] [cube20.org](https://www.cube20.org/)
  - Growth ≈13.35× per move [C]; Kociemba: "about 13.3" [P] [OptimalSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-OptimalSolver).
  - Two generators alone (<R,U>) reach 73,483,200 states [C].
  - CayleyPy frames all such puzzles as Cayley graphs (nodes = states, edges = generator moves) and targets graphs of ~10^100 nodes that "cannot be approached in a usual way". [P] [cayleypy/cayleypy README](https://github.com/cayleypy/cayleypy)
- **(b) Learned distance-to-goal.**
  - Korf: IDA* with admissible PDB lower bounds = exact distances on abstractions. [S] [Korf 1997](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/1997/korf1997aaai-finding)
  - DeepCubeA: cost-to-go learned by approximate value iteration. [S] [DeepCubeA](https://cse.sc.edu/~foresta/assets/files/SolvingTheRubiksCubeWithDeepReinforcementLearningAndSearch_Final.pdf)
  - CayleyPy: diffusion distance (random-walk length) as the target. [P] [cayleypy-cube](https://github.com/khoruzhii/cayleypy-cube)
  - Q*: optimality guaranteed when the learned heuristic does not overestimate. [S] [Q* search](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.04518v2)
- **(c) Phase decomposition.**
  - Thistlethwaite: 4 phases, indices 2,048 / 1,082,565 / 29,400 / 663,552, bounds 7/13/15/17 → 52, later 7/10/13/15 → 45. [S] [Jaap](https://www.jaapsch.net/puzzles/thistle.htm); [S] [Hoey 1992](https://www.math.rwth-aachen.de/~Martin.Schoenert/Cube-Lovers/Dan_Hoey__Re__Rubik's_Cube,_the_minimax_number_of_moves.html); subgroup orders verified [C].
  - Kociemba: 2 phases with 18 moves in phase 1, then 10 G1-preserving moves. [S] [kociemba.org](https://kociemba.org/math/twophase.htm)
  - The God's-number proof reused the same G1 cosets as its unit of work (2,217,093,120 cosets × 19,508,428,800). [S] [cube20.org copy](https://www.cs.brandeis.edu/~storer/JimPuzzles/RUBIK/Rubik3x3x3/READING/GodsNumberIs20.pdf); [C]
- **(d) Search versus one-shot policy.**
  - Two-phase quality improves with time: 20.45 → 18.37 HTM from 0.01 s to 10 s (PyPy). [P] [TwophaseSolver README](https://github.com/hkociemba/RubiksCube-TwophaseSolver)
  - AlphaCube: beam 1,024 → 65,536 gives 21 → 19 moves (small model) and 17 (large model). [P] [alphacube README](https://github.com/kyo-takano/alphacube)
  - CayleyPy uses beam width 2^20. [P] [cayleypy-cube](https://github.com/khoruzhii/cayleypy-cube)
  - DeepCubeA uses weighted A*. [P] [DeepCubeA repo](https://github.com/forestagostinelli/DeepCubeA)
- **Group theory ↔ depth (explicit literature).**
  - Liu, Ash, Goel, Krishnamurthy, Zhang, "Transformers Learn Shortcuts to Automata" (ICLR 2023, arXiv 2210.10749): O(log T)-depth transformer simulators for all automata, and O(1)-depth simulators for automata whose groups are solvable, explained via Krohn–Rhodes theory and circuit complexity. "The only obstructions are when the semiautomata contain non-solvable groups such as S_5." Shallow transformers trained by gradient descent do find these shortcuts, but the shortcuts are "statistically brittle". [S] [arXiv 2210.10749](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.10749); [S] [OpenReview PDF](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=De4FYqjFueZ)
  - 2025 follow-up, "Finite State Automata Inside Transformers with Chain-of-Thought: A Mechanistic Study on State Tracking" (arXiv 2502.20129): transformers with CoT learn group word problems of arbitrary length across several groups, while a Pause-token variant without CoT fails on longer sequences. [S] [arXiv 2502.20129v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.20129v3)
  - Cube-specific LLM benchmarks (CubeBench, Cube Bench, Dec 2025) frame the cube as a long-horizon state-tracking test with exact oracle distance-to-goal. [S] [CubeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23328); [S] [Cube Bench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.20595)

### Inferences
- **The cube group is non-solvable.** Its corner-permutation image is all of S8, which contains A5. This is a standard fact, not fetched; sympy's solvability check on G0 returned "non-solvable" before the slower checks were dropped [C]. By Liu et al., a fixed-depth transformer can at best only approximate cube state-tracking over long move sequences; depth that grows (log T) with sequence length is needed. A looped transformer is the natural way to get that depth without more parameters. This is the strongest theory-backed bridge between "the cube" and "looped models". It concerns *tracking* (forward simulation), not *solving* (inverse search).
- **Concrete mapping table for the report writer:**

  | Cube mechanism | Looped-model counterpart |
  |---|---|
  | Generators | Unique blocks (A, B) |
  | Word / move sequence | Block schedule (A→B→A→B…) |
  | Cayley-graph distance | Loops needed to reach a target representation |
  | Pruning table / learned cost-to-go | Distance-to-go or halting head |
  | Subgroup chain (G0 ⊃ G1 ⊃ … ⊃ {e}) | Stage-wise intermediate targets from the teacher (teacher-internals matching = "reach G1") |
  | Restricted move set after reaching G1 | Later loops use a restricted or cheaper block that must preserve already-solved structure |
  | Symmetry reduction | Weight or cache sharing across input symmetries |
  | Beam or IDA* search | Test-time multiple trajectories or extra loops |
  | Commutator [X,Y] | Block pairs whose composite makes a small, localized correction |

  The rows for halting, path search and permutation matching overlap other researchers' slices; this table only names the cube side.
- **What does not transfer directly.** Cube solvers rely on exact invertibility (every move has an inverse, so random walks from the goal give free labeled data) and on a discrete, exactly checkable goal. Network blocks are neither invertible nor discrete. The "train on walks from the goal" trick (DeepCubeA, Takano, CayleyPy) maps best onto settings where the target representation is known, as in distillation from a teacher's final hidden state, and noise or perturbations are added and then undone.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that explicitly maps Thistlethwaite/Kociemba subgroup phases onto layer, loop or stage structure in neural networks, and none on looped or weight-tied networks for cube solving.
- Merrill, Petty and Sabharwal's "The Illusion of State in State-Space Models" (ICML 2024) is relevant to this bridge: S5/permutation-composition word problems are outside TC0 under standard assumptions. It did not come up in this session's searches and was not verified, so it is not cited here.
- Whether the Liu et al. depth bounds have been tested on the Rubik's cube group specifically (as opposed to S5 and small groups) was not found.
